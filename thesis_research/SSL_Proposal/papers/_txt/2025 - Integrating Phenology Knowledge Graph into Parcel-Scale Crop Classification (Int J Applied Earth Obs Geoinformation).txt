ABSTRACT
Parcels are the fundamental units of agricultural management; accurate crop classification of cropland parcels is 
crucial for the implementation of precision agriculture. Despite extensive knowledge of crop growth processes, 
the difficulty in acquiring this knowledge and its modal differences with remote sensing data hinder its appli-
cation in crop classification research. Moreover, the highly complex and variable growth patterns of crops 
present significant challenges for time-series crop classification. We propose a novel crop classification frame-
work that extracts intricate multi-period features of crop growth from remote sensing time-series signals. 
Additionally, we introduce an automatic construction process for crop remote sensing knowledge graphs based 
on a decision tree structure, capturing the association between crops and remote sensing time-series data. 
Through graph convolution, knowledge graph serves as a global guide to improve crop classification. By 
combining field survey samples with visible, near-infrared, and radar signals, we constructed a parcel-scale 
dataset of rice and wheat crops across four cities in the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River using 
zonal feature aggregation methods for evaluation. The results indicate that the proposed framework achieves 
accuracies ranging from 89.45 % to 94.43 % across the four datasets. We conducted inferences in the four cities 
and compared the results with county-level statistical data, achieving R2 values of 0.89 and 0.97 for wheat and 
rice planting areas, respectively. Our proposed framework can automatically generate crop knowledge graphs 
based on samples from different regions, overcoming the modal barriers between the knowledge space and the 
remote sensing feature space, thus enhancing crop recognition accuracy.
1. Introduction
The pressing necessity to enhance agricultural production efficiency 
and implement precision agricultural management has emerged as a 
pivotal requirement for contemporary agricultural advancement 
(Gebbers et al., 2010; Liaghat et al., 2010). Precision agriculture relies 
on detailed information about cropland, among which crop type infor-
mation is fundamental and crucial (Porter et al., 2014). Accurate crop 
classification is essential for effective precision agriculture practices, as 
different crop types exhibit distinct phenological stages. By accurately 
identifying crop types, targeted monitoring and tailored agricultural 
measures, such as optimized fertilization, irrigation, and pest control, 
can be implemented, thereby enhancing crop yield and quality. 
Numerous studies and practical applications have demonstrated that 
utilizing remote sensing technology for crop classification is a highly 
effective approach (Yang et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2024).
Significant improvements in the temporal and spatial resolution of 
remote sensing data, along with advances in optical and radar imaging 
technology, have propelled the development of crop type mapping. In 
particular, the enhancement in temporal resolution enables more 
frequent and intensive monitoring of crop growth dynamics, thereby 
providing more effective data for remote sensing-based crop classifica-
tion, exemplified by the utilization of Moderate Resolution Imaging 
Spectroradiometer (MODIS) data (Justice et al., 2002; Pan et al., 2012). 
Taking advantage of temporal resolution, (Gusso et al., 2014) proposed 
a Medium Resolution Imaging Crop Detection Algorithm (MCDA) that 
estimates soybean planting area by obtaining dense time series of 
vegetation index from MODIS imagery. Similarly, (Peng et al., 2011) 
introduced an EVI/LSWI threshold function based on dense time series 
from MODIS imagery to identify rice fields in flooded conditions, 
tailored to local agricultural management practices. However, while 
MODIS’s high temporal resolution facilitates multi-temporal crop

To address this limitation, improvements in the spatial resolution of 
Landsat and the Sentinel series satellites launched in 2015 have 
enhanced the detailed representation of crop coverage areas. For 
example, (Tatsumi et al., 2015) developed a multi-crop classification 
method using EVI and its spatial statistical features derived from Landsat 
7 Enhanced Thematic Mapper Plus (Landsat 7 ETM+). Likewise, 
(Kobayashi et al., 2020) calculated various vegetation indices and ana-
lysed the impact of different optical feature combinations from Sentinel- 
2A data on crop classification. However, optical data are susceptible to 
cloud cover, which may result in missing critical information during key 
growing stages of crops (Arias et al., 2020). In contrast, due to the 
advantage of microwave signals in penetrating clouds, SAR data exhibit 
significant potential in regions with pervasive cloud cover. (Xu et al., 
2018) constructed a temporal intensity model using multi-temporal SAR 
backscattering responses and introduced a Spectral Similarity Value 
(SSV) measurement method for crop identification. Furthermore, 
(Chakhar et al., 2021) combined Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2A data to 
analyse the effects of synthetic aperture radar and optical data on crop 
classification.
Supported by abundant remote sensing data (Cao et al., 2022a,b), 
methods for remote sensing crop classification are primarily categorized 
into vegetation index (VI) based methods and machine learning ap-
proaches. For instance, (Chen et al., 2023) developed a Green and Water 
Content Composite Index (GWCCI) to map soybean using NDVI (related 
to greenness) and shortwave infrared (SWIR) bands based on Sentinel-2 
data. The VI-based method allows for intuitive differentiation of crops 
without requiring samples. However, VI-based methods rely on simple 
band calculations derived from local agricultural practices, which limits 
their ability to fully exploit the potential information across multiple 
bands. In contrast, machine learning methods can automatically learn 
complex feature combinations from the data, integrating multiple bands 
and various vegetation indices to thoroughly explore the intricate re-
lationships between remote sensing data and crop type information. 
Consequently, machine learning provides greater flexibility and higher 
accuracy in remote sensing-based crop classification. For instance, 
(Kussul et al., 2017) compared the effectiveness of CNN and MLP ar-
chitectures for crop classification in heterogeneous environments using 
annual Landsat-8 and Sentinel-1A data. Similarly, (Pott et al., 2022) 
employed a random forest model to produce an accurate map of soybean 
and corn using Sentinel-2 time series data. However, machine learning 
models treat data from different periods as independent features, failing 
to automatically capture temporal dependencies. This limitation hinders 
the effective utilization of crop growth temporal patterns in crop 
classification.
Utilizing remote sensing time-series data allows the capture of dy-
namic alterations in crops throughout the growing season and differ-
entiates the distinct phenological characteristics and growth patterns of 
diverse crops. For example, certain crops can be preliminarily identified 
by exploiting key unique time points in the growing process. (Xiao et al., 
2006) employed VI thresholds to identify rice during the flooding and 
transplanting stages based on time series satellite data. However, such 
methods heavily rely on experiential knowledge and fail to fully exploit 
the entire time series, potentially overlooking critical information 
beyond the selected key stages. In recent years, many studies have 
focused on modeling complete time series data. Notably, Recurrent 
Neural Networks (RNNs) have proven to be an effective method for 
temporal pattern recognition. (De Castro et al., 2020) employed LSTM 
and Bi-LSTM models for pattern recognition using Sentinel-1 SAR time 
series data throughout the year to determine rice planting distribution. 
(Xu et al., 2020) developed the DeepCropMapping (DCM) method, 
introducing attention mechanisms into LSTM for dynamic corn and 
soybean mapping. Furthermore, some researchers have also combined 
CNN and RNN to leverage the strengths of both methods. (Zhao et al., 
2020) established a Multi-CNN Sequence-to-Sequence (MCNN-Seq) 
model to define the relationship between optical and SAR time series, 
effectively mining temporal information. Despite these advances, the 
heterogeneous nature of crops across various geographic regions and 
climatic conditions- particularly in growing cycles, phenological stages, 
and maturity- remains a challenge. For instance, rice in Northern China 
grows once a season, whereas in the South, three-season rice is observed. 
Consequently, these methods usually assume a fixed periodicity in the 
time series, hampering the ability to capture complex multi-period 
patterns in crop time series from different regions.
The aforementioned crop classification methods predominantly rely 
on data-driven models, which classify by analyzing substantial volumes 
of remote sensing data and time-series characteristics. In contrast, 
knowledge-driven models are more interpretable and transferable. As a 
structured form of knowledge representation, knowledge graph can 
systematically organize and represent domain expert knowledge 
(Todorovski et al., 2006), such as crop growth characteristics, planting 
patterns, and geographic distribution, providing rich semantic context 
for remote sensing data analysis. For instance, (Peng et al., 2024) 
developed three phenology indicators for mapping tea plantations by 
utilizing knowledge related to tea tree cultivation management practices 
and biochemical substance content. Similarly, (Zhao et al., 2022) con-
structed a phenology parameter map using optimal classification fea-
tures from machine learning, selecting the corresponding best 
classification features based on the available image period and classi-
fying early-stage crops through matrix segmentation. Additionally, 
(Barriere et al., 2024) incorporated contextual data on crop rotation and 
historical survey information as prior knowledge into a stacked LSTM 
model, improving crop classification performance. However, there are 
limitations in current knowledge graph-based crop classification studies. 
First, the knowledge graphs used in these studies are not explicit graph 
structures, which impedes the hierarchical and systematic representa-
tion of remote sensing crop knowledge. Second, the agricultural 
knowledge used in some studies is difficult to acquire and highly 
dependent on complete agricultural survey systems, such as historical 
crop rotation data, which are usually unavailable in most regions. 
Furthermore, traditional knowledge graphs are typically constructed 
from text repositories or corpora, such as online encyclopedias and 
literature. This form of knowledge organization makes it challenging to 
integrate remote sensing information, such as spatial distribution and 
temporal changes in reflectance spectra, thereby impeding the utiliza-
tion of knowledge graphs to directly guide remote sensing crop classi-
fication. Moreover, the challenge lies in the inherent modality 
heterogeneity between the structured organization of knowledge graphs 
and the Euclidean space of remote sensing reflectance spectra, which 
complicates their integration with existing crop classification models. 
Due to the lack of an effective way for knowledge acquisition and 
integration, current knowledge graph-based remote sensing crop clas-
sification methods struggle to break through the barriers between 
knowledge space and remote sensing space, hindering progress in 
addressing the challenges faced in remote sensing crop classification.
To address the challenges faced in the current field of remote sensing 
crop classification, our study proposes a parcel-level multi-modal crop 
classification framework that integrates auto-generated knowledge 
graph into multi-period deep time series modelling, aiming to overcome 
the limitations of existing methods and improve the accuracy, inter-
pretability, and applicability of crop classification. Our work makes the 
following key contributions: 
1. Automated Crop Knowledge Graph Generation: In response to chal-
lenges such as the difficulty in acquiring expert knowledge in remote 
sensing crops and the inability of traditional knowledge graphs to 
incorporate knowledge related to remote sensing signal responses, 
we propose a decision tree parameter learning based method for 
knowledge graph generation. By automatically extracting crop time- 
spectral feature knowledge from the decision tree mechanism, we

2. Materials
2.1. Crop samples and study area
The crop category data involved in this study were derived from field 
sampling, and field surveys were conducted within the study area to 
collect accurate and detailed farmland crop samples. Specifically, the 
main crop categories included in our sample are the two staple food 
crops of wheat and rice, with some other crops included as category 
complements. Crops in the “other” category mainly include non-rice and 
non-wheat samples collected during the survey, such as corn, lotus root, 
soybean, etc. These samples undergo strict quality control and stan-
dardization. Our research area was selected in four cities in the middle 
and lower reaches of the Yangtze River in China, namely Wuhan, 
Nanjing, Jingzhou, and Yichang (Fig. 1), and the number of samples in 
each city is shown in Table 1. These areas are important agricultural 
growing areas for both wheat and rice.
2.2. Earth observation and time series data
This study uses Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 remote sensing data pro-
vided by the European Space Agency’s (ESA) Copernicus program. The 
Sentinel-1 satellite is equipped with a C-band synthetic aperture radar 
(SAR), which enables the acquisition of surface information under all- 
weather, all-day conditions, particularly excelling in cloud-covered 
areas. The Sentinel-2 satellite carries a multispectral imager (MSI) that 
provides high-resolution optical imagery. To reduce the impact of cloud 
interference and data gaps, we applied median synthesis to the data 
from Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 to obtain monthly temporal data. The 
preprocessing of earth observation data was conducted on the Google 
Earth Engine (GEE) platform, utilizing atmospherically corrected data-
sets for image filtering, cloud removal, index calculation, and data 
downloading.
For image feature selection, we selected four spectral bands (R, G, B, 
NIR) from the Sentinel-2 imagery and computed the NDVI and LSWI 
indices. The NDVI index is sensitive to vegetation growth conditions and 
chlorophyll content, effectively distinguishing between vegetated and 
non-vegetated areas, while reflecting the intensity of photosynthesis and 
biomass (Pettorelli et al., 2005). The LSWI, on the other hand, is a good 
indicator of vegetation water content and soil moisture (Chandrasekar 
et al., 2010), reflecting the moisture status and drought resistance of 
vegetation, which is especially useful for identifying crops like rice in 
paddy fields.

2.3. Parcel-scale data processing
We chose parcel as the unit of analysis for crop mapping primarily 
because crops are typically cultivated at the parcel level, and within a 
single parcel, the growth environment and planting patterns are more 
consistent, exhibiting higher homogeneity. The parcel data is sourced 
from (Zhu et al., 2023), who used high-resolution imagery and multi- 
level edge-aware deep learning techniques to obtain high-resolution 
agricultural parcel vectors. The cropland parcels are shown in Fig. 1.
For feature processing at the parcel scale, we applied three zonal 
statistics methods—Mean, Median, and Variance—on the pixels within 
the parcel boundaries based on the final selection of 11 bands. These 
methods comprehensively capture the spectral and texture characteris-
tics of the parcel. The mean provides an overall representation of the 
parcel, reflecting the average levels of each band and index. The median, 
as a robust measure of central tendency, offers a more accurate 
representation of the parcel’s typical characteristics in the presence of 
outliers. The variance quantifies the internal heterogeneity of the parcel, 
helping to identify complex or heterogeneous agricultural structures. 
The combined use of these three statistical metrics not only provides 
complementary information but also enhances the robustness and 
adaptability of the feature extraction process.

3.3, we will delve into the deep learning time series classification model

3.1. Knowledge graph automatic extraction using decision trees
To reduce the difficulty of acquiring remote sensing crop knowledge 
graphs, we propose an automated knowledge extraction method that 
mines key crop recognition features from decision tree structures and 
links them with temporal information. This approach facilitates the 
construction of a remote sensing crop knowledge graph. Traditional 
knowledge graphs typically represent knowledge as triplets (Lin et al., 
2015), consisting of a subject (head), relation, and object (tail), 
expressed as (head, relation, tail). The knowledge graph we constructed 
adheres to the triplet form of (key recognition features, time, crop type). 
(See Fig. 3).
Specifically, we employed a random forest model to train the dataset. 
Through cross-validation and parameter optimization, we achieved 
performance above the baseline. Inspired by the tree structure of 
random forests, we employed a node selection approach based on the 
Gini coefficient, selecting features from decision nodes and matching 
them with their corresponding high-probability inference results. In 
decision trees, the selection and division of each decision node are based 
on specific criteria, one of which is the Gini coefficient. The Gini coef-
ficient, commonly used to measure dataset purity, is calculated as fol-

3.2. Knowledge graph embedding based on graph convolution network
As a heterogeneous graph structure with edge information, knowl-

(Chen et al., 2020). Compared to the sparsity and computational diffi-

Networks (GNNs), which have emerged in recent years, inherently 
possess strong representation capabilities. The goal of GNNs is to learn a 
state embedding that represents the neighborhood information of each 
node (Scarselli et al., 2008). The state embedding hv can generate output 
vectors Ov, which can be directly used as the distribution for predicting 
node labels or for deep representation of graph data.

the automatically constructed knowledge graph. Unlike traditional 
GNNs, R-GCNs are more suitable for feature embedding of relational 
data. The model’s motivation is to extend GCN to relational data by 
using local neighborhood information in the graph. The embedding

3.3. Multi-period deep time series modelling
Although it is commonly assumed that crops within a certain region 
follow a uniform phenological cycle, the actual delineation of these 
cycles can be variable. This variability is particularly evident in remote 
sensing reflectance curves. Differences in soil properties, moisture and 
other factors between parcels can lead to diverse growth patterns. Uti-
lizing wheat samples collected in Wuhan and Nanjing, we plotted the 
NDVI curves throughout the growing stages in these two regions (Fig. 6). 
Significant differences were observed between the NDVI profiles of 
wheat in Nanjing and Wuhan. In Nanjing, wheat follows a relatively 
simple phenological pattern with stages including early stage, greening, 
jointing, heading, and maturing. This straightforward division into five 
stages can effectively distinguish wheat types based on the NDVI vari-
ation curve. Conversely, the phenological pattern of wheat in Wuhan is 
more complex, with the NDVI variation curve exhibiting two peaks. The 
first peak corresponds to the stages of sowing-emergence-tillering- 
overwintering, while the second peak aligns with regreening-jointing- 
heading-maturity. In this scenario, a more granular division of the 
remote sensing feature changes is required for accurate wheat 
identification.
Within the same region, the specific location of farmland parcels can 
result in significant differences in their remote sensing phenological 
curves, diverging from the typical local phenological pattern. For 
instance, wheat in parcel A on a plain riverbank, benefiting from optimal 
geographical and microclimate conditions, adheres to a simpler growth 
pattern. In contrast, wheat in parcel B on terraced mountainous land, 
which undergoes overwintering, conforms to a more complex multi- 
stage growth pattern. This indicates that each farmland parcel may 
possess unique environmental conditions, leading to distinct remote 
sensing phenological response curves and different cyclical patterns. 
Consequently, this variability undermines the direct applicability of 
standard phenological models.
Thus, it is imperative to uncover the multi-cyclical patterns 
embedded within remote sensing feature curves. To achieve this, we 
developed a deep time series model capable of extracting multi-cyclical 
features. This model decouples the multi-cyclical phenological calendar 
present in crop remote sensing curves, offering inherent advantages for 
crop classification and demonstrating superior capability in modelling 
dependencies within and between cycles.
In remote sensing time series, only scalar data of several features are 
saved at a single moment, and more information is contained in time 
series changes. While it is often assumed that crops in a region share a 
similar phenological cycle, the division of these cycles is not always so 
fixed. This variability is particularly evident in remote sensing reflec-
tance curves, which are influenced by differences in soil properties, 
moisture content, and other factors between different cropland parcels. 
Consequently, the crop growth cycle may exhibit various patterns. For 
example, in Fig. 7(a), wheat in parcel A may adhere to a 2-stage growing 
pattern, whereas wheat in parcel B may follow a 4-stage growth model. 
Therefore, it is essential to explore the multi-period patterns in the 
reflectance curves. Motivated by (Wu et al., 2022), we adopted a deep 
time series model capable of extracting multi-period features of crops in 
remote sensing time series data. This model has inherent advantages 
when applied to crop classification, demonstrating exceptional ability to 
model dependency patterns both within and between periods.

3.4. Knowledge graph-embedded multi-period time series crop 
classification
This study proposes a deep time series crop classification framework 
integrated with knowledge graphs. In the remote sensing time series 
classification task, since different crops have unique phenology condi-
tions and growth cycles, the correlation pattern between remote sensing 
feature information and crop categories is very complex. Reliance on 
time series models alone may not fully exploit all available information. 
Especially in many practical applications, time series data can often be 
associated with some external knowledge. In our study, external 
knowledge exists in the form of a knowledge graph, and the nodes and 
edges in the graph represent the relationships between time, crops, and 
remote sensing features respectively. To fully utilize the knowledge 
graph, we design a fusion mechanism to combine the time series model 
with the embedding generated by R-GCN to improve crop classification 
performance.
First, we encode the input time series using a Fourier transform- 
based multi-period time series model, which captures the complex pe-
riodic variation relationships in the time series through a multi-period 
mapping mechanism. The temporal encoder generates a sequence of 
hidden states H={h1, h2,..., hT}. These hidden states contain rich in-
formation in time series, especially the periodic characteristics of crop 
growth. 
H= MultiPeriodTimeSeriesModel(X)
On this basis, we further extract the last hidden state hfinal as a global 
representation of time series features: 
hfinal = HT

R-GCN model generates the embedding of each node by considering 
different types of relationship edges in the graph. In this process, we 
select node embedding zKG related to time series tasks as the global 
representation of the knowledge graph.

3.5. Experiment settings
3.5.1. Environment
The TSKG models of four cities are trained on a server with NVIDIA 
RTX 4060 graphics processing unit (GPU) accelerators (with 8-GB 
memory), and were implemented in PyTorch 1.2.0, and Python 3.8.
3.5.2. Parameter settings
For TSKG and other deep learning models, the epoch is uniformly set 
to 150, the learning rate is 1e-3, the Adam optimizer is used, and the 
batch size is 16. For Random Forest, the number of trees is set to 200 
after parameter grid search optimization. To evaluate different methods, 
we divided the dataset into train set, test set and val set according to the 
ratio of 6:2:2.
4. Results
4.1. Qualification and quantitative results on parcel-scale crop dataset
Fig. 8 shows the results of different time series crop classification 
methods on the parcel time series data set. The positions marked with 
red dots in satellite images are verification samples collected on the spot. 
The verification curve shows the comparison between the NDVI curve of 
the verification sample and the crop standard curve. We employed 
Random Forest (Breiman, 2001), Transformer (Vaswani, 2017), 
Informer (Zhou et al., 2021), and DLinear (Zeng et al., 2023) as 
comparative methods. Random Forest is a widely used machine learning 
algorithm for crop classification (Sheykhmousa et al., 2020), while 
Transformer serves as a foundational sequence learning model. Informer 
enhances Transformer with sparse computation improvements, and 
DLinear is a simple yet efficient algorithm for time-series learning.
As can be seen from Fig. 8, the TSKG method has high consistency 
and accuracy in time series crop classification. The red dots in the sat-
ellite imagery represent the locations of validation samples. The vali-
dation curve compares the NDVI curves of these validation samples with 
the standard crop curves. Case#1 and Case#4 show a continuous rice 
planting area. It can be seen from the phenological curve that the crop 
type of the parcel where the verification point is located is rice. The 
TSKG method can accurately classify, while other methods will cause a 
certain degree of misjudgment. In Case #2, for other crops other than 
rice and wheat, TSKG can also accurately judge the planting situation of 
the parcel. In Case #3, in an obvious wheat planting area, TSKG can 
ensure the consistency and accuracy of classification, while the other 
methods have a certain degree of misclassification. Furthermore, the 
figure delineates the relationship between the knowledge graph and 
phenological period. For example, in Case#1, the phenological knowl-
edge depicted in the knowledge graph aligns with the remote sensing 
features observed in the validation samples and standard time series, 
underscoring the guiding role of the knowledge graph in crop 
classification.
Table 3 shows the classification accuracy of different methods on 
four urban parcel-scale crop data sets. OA classified by TSKG performed 
best on the data sets of each city, with OA reaching 94.43 %, 91.26 %, 
89.45 %, and 92.91 % respectively, which was significantly higher than 
RF. Because traditional machine learning models such as RF are difficult 
to handle the implicit temporal relationships between data features, 
their accuracy is relatively low. The structure of DLinear network might 
be too concise to learn complex growing patterns of crops, leading to 
lower accuracy than RF in some cases. Compared with Transformer and 
Informer, who usually view time series data as fixed length series, TSKG 
can better handle the multi-period patterns implicit in time series and 
globally guide crop classification by integrating knowledge graph fea-
tures, achieving the best overall performance among these models.

5. Discussion
The knowledge graph fused deep time series crop classification 
framework proposed in this study has made progress in the field of 
remote sensing crop classification. Through automated knowledge 
graph construction, application of graph convolution neural network 
technology and multi-period deep time series modelling, crop classifi-
cation has been effectively improved in accuracy, interpretability, and 
applicability.
The proposed framework has good applicability in crop classification 
tasks. It outperforms other models such as RF, Transformer, Informer, 
and DLinear by effectively handling multi-period patterns in time series 
and incorporating knowledge graph features to globally guide crop 
classification, achieving the best classification accuracy. By automating 
the construction of remote sensing crop knowledge maps, we effectively 
avoid the cumbersome process of relying on manual expert knowledge, 
which is especially important for large-scale and dynamically changing 
remote sensing data sets. In traditional methods, expert knowledge often 
requires in-depth domain knowledge and long-term accumulation, 
while our knowledge graph construction method can automatically 
extract the relationship between crop growth stages and remote sensing 
features through decision trees, which is in the absence of support from 
domain experts. Unlike fixed parameters of knowledge (Zhao et al., 
2022), graph neural networks, as a flexible structured data modelling 
method, can dynamically encode knowledge to best fit the crop classi-
fication. Specifically, R-GCN effectively combines remote sensing time 
series data with knowledge graphs, further enhancing the model’s 
adaptability to crop growth patterns in complex geographical environ-
ments and improving classification accuracy.
By combining time series data, knowledge graphs, and deep learning 
models, the framework can simultaneously capture crop growth 
knowledge and temporal change characteristics, overcoming the limi-
tations of single-modal data. This method is especially suitable for areas 
with high geographical information diversity, such as different climate 
zones or agricultural areas. Compared to knowledge from a developed 
agricultural census system (Barriere et al., 2024), knowledge graph can 
be easily updated by the auto-extraction at low cost with local data. It 
can be adaptively adjusted according to different growth cycles and

Our study classifies crops on the parcel scale and extracts features 
such as mean, median, and variance through zonal calculation of parcel 
vectors. However, the accuracy of the parcel vector can vary signifi-
cantly in practical applications. If the vector data of cropland parcels is 
not accurate enough, it may lead to the inclusion of redundant non- 
agricultural areas or the omission of some crop growth areas in the 
calculation process, thus affecting the accuracy of statistical feature 
extraction. Especially in the edge areas of the parcel, the mixed pixel 
effect may lead to deviations in the spectral characteristics of the remote 
sensing band and affect the performance of the classification model. In 
areas where cropland is severely fragmented, the parcel shape is com-
plex, or the area is small, the statistical features extracted by zonal 
calculation of the parcel vector may not fully represent the growth of the 
entire crop, affecting the classification effect of the model. In this case, in 
the future, we can try to introduce high-precision parcel vector data or 
combine it with automatic parcel segmentation methods to improve the 
accuracy of parcel boundaries.
This study used 6 common spectral bands and 5 SAR bands in the 
selection of remote sensing bands. Although these bands can provide 
basic spectral and structural characteristics of crops, they may not be 
able to capture more detailed crops. Spectral reflectance characteristics, 
especially at certain critical stages of crop growth, specific narrow 
bands, or thermal infrared bands, may contribute more to crop classi-
fication. With the rapid development of remote sensing sensor tech-
nology, new hyperspectral sensors and multi-polarization SAR sensors 
can provide richer and more detailed spectral and structural informa-
tion. Future research can explore more complex band combinations, 
such as the rich narrow bands in hyperspectral data or multi-temporal, 
multi-polarization SAR data, to dig deeper into crop remote sensing 
characteristics and enhance the accuracy and stability of classification.
In addition, although multi-modal fusion can improve classification 
accuracy, its effect is also strongly affected by data quality and pre-
processing methods. In practical applications, remote sensing data are 
usually interfered by clouds, image occlusion, noise, and other factors. 
Low data quality may lead to reduced effectiveness of knowledge graphs 
and models. In order to fully realize the advantages of multi-modal 
fusion, it is necessary to ensure the strict execution of data acquisi-
tion, preprocessing, and quality control, which is crucial to the gener-
alization ability and accuracy of the model.
6. Conclusion
This study develops a parcel-level multi-modal crop classification 
framework that integrates auto-generated knowledge graph into multi- 
period deep time series modelling (TSKG), aiming to overcome the 
limitations of existing remote sensing crop classification methods in 
terms of accuracy, interpretability, and applicability. Through the tree- 
based structure, we automatically constructed a remote sensing crop 
knowledge map. By introducing graph neural networks, we achieved 
dynamic embedding of knowledge. With multi-period time series 
modelling, implicit growing patterns of crops are captured to improve 
the performance of crop classification. The classification performance is 
evaluated using independent ground truth data and agricultural census 
data, and compared with other common time series models. The results 
show that the TSKG method has better accuracy than the comparative 
methods on the crop datasets. From the visual effect and time series 
comparison, the results of TSKG are more consistent and accurate. In 
addition, the estimated crop area of TSKG agrees well with county-level 
agricultural census data. After adding the feature guidance of the 
knowledge map, the boundaries of crop classification are clearer, and 
the consistency of features of the same crop is higher. Overall, TSKG 
performs well on time series crop classification. This method explores 
the combination of knowledge graphs and remote sensing classification, 
and has great potential for knowledge-driven remote sensing time series 
classification in more areas.
