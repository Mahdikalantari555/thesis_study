Abstract
Agriculture forms the backbone of the global economy, facing mounting pressure from population
growth and resource constraints. The sector increasingly relies on data-driven technologies to
enhance productivity while reducing environmental impact. Agriculture is being revolutionized by
Artificial Intelligence (AI), which is enhancing pesticide application, weed control, and irrigation
management. Deep Learning techniques that have demonstrated predictive power include
Generative Adversarial Networks, Recurrent Neural Networks, and Convolutional Neural Networks.
Their opacity and intricacy, however, make practical use difficult. In agricultural settings, Explainable
AI (XAI) enables informed decisions by providing transparency without compromising perfor-
mance. This comprehensive review analyzes peer- reviewed publications from 2020 onwards,
categorizing XAI techniques and their applications in agriculture. The starting point of 2020 was
deliberately chosen to capture the most recent advancements, as this period marks a phase of rapid
growth and wider adoption of XAI within agricultural AI applications, making it particularly relevant
for reflecting state-of-the-art developments. This review identifies significant challenges, current
research trends, methodological approaches, and evaluate the efficacy of various explainability
methods, including LIME, SHAP, Grad-CAM, and rule-based models. The analysis examines key
domains including crop-weed discrimination, plant disease detection, precision farming techniques,
yield forecasting, and soil quality assessment. The integration of XAI methodologies in precision
agriculture presents promising opportunities to address pressing challenges related to resource
optimization, climate adaptation, and global food security. This review also provides a structured
framework for future research directions and practical implementation guidelines to enhance the
interpretability, trustworthiness, and adoption of AI-powered agricultural systems among farmers,
agronomists, and policymakers.

1.Introduction
Agriculture—the foundation of food, fiber, and commodity production through soil cultivation—confronts
numerous critical challenges in the contemporary era. Disease outbreaks, diminishing land and water
resources, climate change impacts, and persistent pest infestations collectively threaten global agricultural
systems. With projections indicating the need for a 70% increase in agricultural output to meet rising demands,
the sector stands at a crucial juncture. The implementation of intelligent farming methodologies has become
imperative to achieve sustainability goals, strengthen food security frameworks, enhance production efficiency,
and significantly reduce environmental footprints. These sophisticated approaches represent a necessary
evolution in agricultural practice, balancing productivity requirements with ecological responsibility to address
the complex challenges of feeding a growing global population while preserving natural resources for future
generations.
1.1.OverviewofAIinagriculture
The field of Artificial Intelligence (AI) within computer science is growing at a remarkable pace, and its
influence is strongly felt in agriculture—a sector that employs nearly one-third (30.7%) of the global workforce
and covers 2781 million hectares of cultivated land. Across this vast landscape, AI has emerged as a powerful
ally, offering solutions to some of the most pressing challenges in farming. From disease identification and pest
management to precision chemical application, drainage and irrigation optimization, weed control, and crop
yield forecasting [1], AI has consistently proven its value. These systems have shown exceptional accuracy and
reliability in managing complex agricultural scenarios, with an unmatched ability to detect and analyze the
subtle details that define diverse farming conditions [2]. One striking example lies in precise spraying, where
weed detection plays a crucial role in reducing environmental damage, cutting costs, and minimizing chemical
use. This is an area where AI—particularly through ML and DL—has made significant progress. Earlier, weed
species in agricultural fields were identified using image processing algorithms that extracted textural and
morphological features. However, these methods required manual feature extraction and often struggled with
challenges such as varying field conditions, changing illumination, and occlusion. DL models have since
revolutionized this domain, offering far greater accuracy and adaptability than traditional machine learning
approaches. Yet, challenges remain, including limited datasets, environmental variability, and class imbalance.
Overcoming these hurdles and building scalable, dependable AI-powered precision spraying systems is critical
for ensuring sustainable weed management [3].
AI plays a crucial role in irrigation management, integrating soil moisture sensor data, crop growth stages,
and weather forecasts to optimize water distribution. ML models predict irrigation needs, reducing water
wastage and ensuring adequate hydration, supporting sustainable farming practices, especially in regions
facing water scarcity.
AI’s transformative potential is also evident in the realm of crop yield prediction and sustainable farming
practices. By harnessing vast amounts of data, AI empowers farmers to make informed decisions, optimize
resources, and reduce production costs. Tools such as satellite imaging and remote sensing supply extensive
information on crop health, soil quality, and weather conditions. ML models, trained on historical yield data
and environmental factors, then use this knowledge to forecast future harvests. With the ability to monitor
numerous yield-related variables, process large datasets at speed, and generalize across crops and geographies,
AI provides timely insights and early detection of potential risks. These predictive capabilities not only enhance
output but also lower costs and strengthen sustainability, paving the way for more resilient and secure farming
systems [4, 5].
Beyond precision spraying and irrigation management, AI is also being integrated into broader a agri-
cultural systems to enhance productivity and sustainability. AI-driven solutions support tasks such as

1.2.Thefocusofagriculturalaionexplainability
ML and DL algorithms in agriculture are beneficial but often lack transparency, leading to concerns about
safety, fairness, and ethics. Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) aims to address these issues by making AI
models more transparent, fostering better understanding, sound judgment, and increased confidence in AI
systems. This has led to a rise in XAI’s importance across various scientific fields.
XAI techniques can be categorized into three main groups based on methodological generality, explanation
scope, and model selection approach. For model selection, practitioners can choose between model-based
methods (simpler but requiring more sophisticated algorithms) and post-hoc methods. Both model-specific
and model-agnostic XAI approaches offer the flexibility to generate either local explanations (for individual
predictions) or global explanations (for overall model behavior) [11, 12].
Figure 2 illustrates different concepts of XAI as applied in the agricultural sector.
1.3.Motivationofreviewpaper
Despite its potential, there is a noticeable gap in the literature regarding the application of XAI in agriculture.
There is a lack of comprehensive studies summarizing the various XAI techniques employed in agricultural
tasks, as well as limited research on how XAI can enhance specific applications, such as weed detection, disease
classification, and yield prediction. This review aims to bridge this gap by providing a structured analysis of XAI
applications in agriculture, highlighting key methodologies, challenges, and future research directions. By
addressing the interpretability limitations of DL models, this study seeks to advance the integration of XAI-
driven solutions in precision agriculture for improved efficiency, sustainability, and decision-making.
1.4.Objectivesofreviewpaper
This paper reviews the research efforts in the agricultural domain that leverage XAI techniques. The review
spans various key areas, including crop-weed detection, leaf disease identification, precision agriculture, yield
prediction, and soil health monitoring. These domains represent critical facets of modern agriculture where
XAI methods have been employed to enhance the interpretability and reliability of AI-driven solutions. This
review paper aims to consolidate existing research, identify gaps, and provide insights into future directions for
integrating XAI into agricultural AI systems.

meaningful outputs. Its applications extend across numerous fields, including intelligent information retrieval,
expert systems, robotics, automated programming, and perception-based challenges [15]. In agriculture
specifically, AI is transforming traditional practices throughout the entire cultivation cycle. Machine learning
techniques are used for soil assessment, crop management, decision support, aerial monitoring, automation,
plant health, and weed control. AI-assisted machinery improves yield and monitoring, while predictive

3.XAItechniques
XAI focuses on improving the transparency and accountability of AI by creating methods that make AI systems
easier for users to understand, trust, and manage [22, 23]. In simpler terms, XAI explains how AI models make
decisions by identifying the features that influence their predictions. For example, in plant disease detection,
XAI can highlight the specific areas of a leaf image that the model analyzes, helping users verify that the system is
focusing on the correct parts of the plant.
3.1.OverviewofXAI
Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) are sophisticated artificial intelligence models that improve transparency in
high-stakes situations like precision medicine and driverless cars [24]. They have complex black-box models
and strong learning algorithms. Through strong learning performance and explainable models, XAI principles
seek to make AI comprehensible and trustworthy for people, guaranteeing accuracy and dependability across a
range of industries, including agriculture [25]. A general overview of XAI is shown in figure 5.
3.2.GoalsofXAI
XAI aims to enhance transparency, interpretability, and reliability of AI systems by providing clear
explanations, ensuring consistency, and promoting error identification. It also focuses on fairness, bias
detection, and ethical compliance.
• Trustworthiness: The XAI model aims to ensure trustworthiness, ensuring that the model will behave as
planned when faced with an obstacle. However, not every reliable model can be explained by itself, and

perturbations. Establishing best practices for assessing interpretation techniques and their justifications
including human-computer interaction presents a difficulty [35].
3.3.Interpretablemodelsversusexplainablemodels
(1) Explainable models: A post-hoc knowledge of the model’s decision-making or prediction process is made
possible by post hoc analysis using additional tools and careful study by subject-matter experts.
(2) Interpretable models: The ante-hoc model design and domain expertise allow experts in the field to swiftly
and easily comprehend the model’s prediction or decision-making process [36].
To comprehend how intelligent systems make decisions, interpretability, and explainability are frequently
used synonymously in literature. Explainability is connected to the internal logic and mechanics of a learning
system, whereas interpretability is concerned with describing or expressing something in a form that is under-
standable to humans. While both are crucial for AI systems, interpretability is what gives humans meaningful
descriptions. Both interpretability and completeness are included in explainability; completeness is defined as a
precise description of how a machine learning model comes to its conclusions. Explainable models are always
interpretable, however, not all interpretable models are explainable because of their incompleteness [37].

3.4.CommonXAItechniques
Table 2 provides the definitions of various XAI approaches and comparisons between them, and table 3
categorizes XAI approaches based on their interpretability properties, computational costs, open-source
tools, and agricultural data types, illustrating both theoretical distinctions and practical deployment.
SHAP and SmoothGrad are computationally intensive, LightGBM has a low computational cost, and
Grad- CAM is moderate. Grad-CAM and SmoothGrad are well-suited for image-based tasks, while
SHAP, LIME, and LightGBM are ideal for tabular data. SHAP, LIME, and Grad-CAM enhance
interpretability in crop–weed classification, whereas LightGBM and SHAP are particularly effective for
crop recommender systems, offering transparency and feature relevance insights. XAI adoption in
agricultural AI systems depends on model type, data modality, research goals, and practical constraints.
Vision-based tasks often use model-specific visualization methods like Grad-CAM, while sensor and
tabular studies use model- agnostic approaches like SHAP and LIME. Technique selection in agricultural
applications is influenced by end-user preferences, computational efficiency, scalability, and interpret-
ability-usability trade-offs.
3.5.EvaluationmetricforXAI
• Sensitivity: Sensitivity analysis is an essential method in XAI that evaluates modifications to machine
learning models such as Grad-CAM, SHAP, and LIME, assisting researchers in comprehending their
robustness and dependability.
• Faithfulness Correlation and Faithfulness Estimate: With a high correlation signifying accurate identifica-
tion and a low correlation signifying underfitting or overfitting, the statistic assesses how well XAI algorithms
identify patterns and relationships in training data.
• Monotonicity Metric: The Monotonicity Metric is a tool that measures model performance progressively
and generates contrastive explanations using monotonic attribute functions.
• Selectivity: In XAI models, selectivity is a statistic that evaluates the decline in the prediction function
following the removal of highly attributed features.
• Sensitivity: Sensitivity-N determines the relationship between the target output variation and the
attribution sum.
• Infidelity: The estimated mean square error between attribution and input perturbation and the model
output difference following a large perturbation is measured by infidelity [42].
3.6.TaxonomyofXAITechniques
• Techniques for White Box and Black Box Model: White box models are transparent and easy to understand
[43] after training but struggle with larger datasets with intricate connections [44]. Black box models, like
decision trees and Neural Networks, have intricate topologies and are challenging to understand due to their
ambiguous function in the prediction model [45]. Both models have limitations regarding complexity and
their ability to describe complex datasets.
• Model-Specific Techniques and Model-Agnostic Techniques: Model-specific techniques are explana-
tion methods designed for a particular type of model because they rely on its internal structure. For
example, Grad-CAM is used with convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to highlight which parts of a
plant image a model focuses on when detecting diseases, helping farmers see the affected areas clearly.
On the other hand, model-agnostic techniques can work with any model because they only use inputs
and outputs without needing to know how the model works inside. For example, SHAP has been
applied in agriculture to understand which factors, like rainfall, soil health, or fertilizer use, are most
important in predicting crop yield. This way, model-specific techniques are ideal for tasks like image-
based disease detection, while model-agnostic methods are better for analyzing patterns in
agricultural data.
• Global Interpretation and Local Interpretation: Decision-making processes are grouped into local
interpretation and global interpretation. Defining variables, dependencies, and interactions within a model
requires a deeper analysis called global interpretation. Local interpretation focuses on the immediate vicinity
of a data point, analyzing particular predictions and decisions. Techniques like Shapley value and LIME can
help comprehend contextual relevance [44].

4.ApplicationsofXAIinagriculture
Tables 1, 4, and 5 provide a detailed overview of various XAI techniques, tools, and frameworks applied in the
agricultural domain, along with their corresponding limitations and the datasets used.
From this analysis, it is evident that XAI has primarily been applied to classification tasks in agriculture,
with additional use cases in urban vegetation mapping, plant phenotyping, and crop recommendation systems.
Among the various methods, LIME and Grad-CAM emerge as the most frequently used techniques, followed
by SHAP, LightGBM, and SmoothGrad.
However, several limitations have been identified. A key concern is the limited generalizability of findings
to other agricultural datasets, as many studies fail to address potential biases present in their datasets. Further-
more, the real-world applicability of these models in practical farming scenarios remains largely unexplored.
Another pressing challenge is the need for large, well-annotated datasets to ensure the robustness and scal-
ability of AI models. Additionally, while SHAP has proven effective for interpretability, it is often computation-
ally slow on large datasets, underlining the importance of developing more efficient techniques.
Recent advancements have also explored cross-modal approaches to better process and interpret agri-
cultural data. For example, the ITF-WPI cross-modal feature fusion model, which integrates CoTN and ODLS,
uses Pyramid Squeezed Attention to enhance a 4-stage network and leverages Transformer-based contextual
feature extraction. Compared to similar models, the ODLS network has demonstrated strong text feature
acquisition with a 30% reduction in MACCs, illustrating its efficiency and potential for practical applications
such as wolfberry pest management and other agricultural use cases [46].
4.1.Cropdiseasedetection
Crop disease detection is being transformed by the integration of AI and DL addressing the rising global
demand for food and the need to protect agricultural production. Recent studies demonstrate that Explainable
AI (XAI) methods can achieve remarkable performance, with one approach identifying 38 plant diseases with
99.69% accuracy, precision, and recall using LIME-based visual explanations [47]. CNN architectures such as
EfficientNetB0 have been successfully applied to banana plant disease detection [48], while EfficientNetV2S
attained 91% accuracy in identifying grapevine downy mildew symptoms in field conditions by detecting tiny

For detecting agricultural diseases in Internet of Things (IoT) edge environments, RepAgrViT is intro-
duced as a lightweight vision transformer architecture. It incorporates an efficient parameter fusion strategy,
integrates CNN features with vision transformer concepts, and employs local-global attention mechanisms.
Additionally, the model leverages neighborhood-based local search strategies and adaptive weighted delayed
velocity optimization. Experimental findings demonstrate that RepAgrViT effectively captures discriminative
disease features, making it suitable for resource-constrained agricultural monitoring systems [52].
A multi-level deep information feature fusion extraction network (DFN-PSAN) is proposed for plant dis-
ease classification in natural field scenarios. This network processes multi-level depth information and empha-
sizes key regions of plant disease images through pyramidal squeezed attention (PSA) and multiple
convolutional layers. The proposed approach achieved strong accuracy and improved model attention trans-
parency, demonstrating competitive performance [53].
The study also presents PPLC-Net, a deep learning model designed to enhance feature extraction and
expand sample diversity using meteorological data augmentation. It addresses spatial information challenges
through GAP layers, a lightweight CBAM attention mechanism, and sawtooth dilated convolution. The model
achieved an F1 score of 98.442% and a recognition accuracy of 99.702%, fulfilling the criteria for precise and
efficient recognition. Furthermore, the integrated CAM-based visualization method validated its interpret-
ability [54].
To support crop disease diagnosis, the article proposes YOLO V5-CAcT, an optimized detection frame-
work built on the lightweight YOLO V5 backbone. The network incorporates Repeated Augmentation, Focal-
Loss, and SmoothBCE for improved robustness and convergence. The model is optimized using simplified
operators and trained through two technical pathways, reducing model size and inference time while achieving
94.24% average recognition accuracy across 59 crop disease categories [55].
4.2.Weeddetectionandclassification
In weed detection and classification, XAI has been integrated with advanced deep learning models to improve
accuracy, interpretability, and real-time applicability. A high-resolution attention architecture based on ResNet
layers was proposed to enhance the localization of weed patches, enabling transparent comparisons across weed
species and growth stages through self-attention maps [29]. This approach maintains accuracy from deeper
layers while improving resolution through shallower ones, thereby supporting cross-dataset evaluations.
Similarly, a modified YOLOv8-nano model was developed for edge device deployment, leveraging the
CottonWeedDet12 dataset with 16,944 images to detect 12 cotton weed classes [56]. The incorporation of a
convolutional block attention module and C3Ghost block reduced computational costs while maintaining
reliability, and interpretability was further enhanced using XAI techniques such as Layer-CAM, Grad-CAM+
+, and Eigen-CAM, which provided visual explanations of weed detection outcomes.
4.3.Precisionagriculture
Crop recommendation systems have greatly improved in terms of performance and interpretability because of
XAI approaches. A study [64] suggested a recommender model that improved decision-making transparency
with an accuracy of 98.2%. The XAI-CROP framework, which was created using datasets from Indian crop
farming, performed better than conventional models and provided convincing arguments for its suggestions
[65]. With excellent scalability across many crops and areas, this improves food security, transparency, and
resource utilization. Predictive maintenance in smart agricultural facilities has also incorporated XAI, which
offers interpretable insights for data, models, and end users. This has improved ROC-AUC, accuracy, and F1-
score in comparison to current methods [66].
4.4.Yieldprediction
In precision agriculture, XAI techniques are increasingly being employed to enhance crop prediction and
recommendation systems. A Bayesian interpretable machine learning model has been proposed for yield
prediction under future climate conditions, outperforming black-box models such as MARS, SVM, neural
networks, Random Forest, and XGBoost by capturing structural discontinuities and nonlinear effects of
temperature and precipitation [67]. Similarly, the XAI-CROP approach, developed using Python, Pandas,
Scikit-learn, and LIME, improves transparency and reliability in crop recommendation systems in America
[68]. Other studies have applied explainable boosting machines for cotton yield prediction, highlighting the
role of key input features [69], while satellite imagery-based models have been used to quantify input
contributions, track crop growth stages, and analyze production variability with feature attribution methods
[70]. These advancements demonstrate how XAI improves interpretability, decision-making, and trust in
agricultural prediction systems.

4.5.Soilhealthmonitoring
XAI has also been applied to soil health monitoring, particularly for optimizing cultivation practices. For rice

accuracy while considering climate conditions, thereby supporting sustainable operations through soil quality

demonstrate their potential for strengthening global food security [72].
4.6.Performancemetrictrendsinreviewedliterature
It is clear from the examined literature that, depending on the task, researchers use a variety of evaluation
criteria. Although accuracy is still the most frequently reported metric, several studies now incorporate
precision, recall, and F1-score to more thoroughly represent performance at the class level. Furthermore,
research that uses regression-oriented methodologies, such growth modeling or yield prediction, usually
reports error-based metrics like the coefficient of determination (R2), mean squared error (MSE), and mean
absolute error (MAE). Direct comparison between research is difficult due to the variation in reported
indicators, but it also highlights how complex performance evaluation is in agricultural AI applications.
Crucially, accuracy alone might not adequately convey a model’s robustness or field-readiness, especially in situ
ations where balancing error rates (F1-score) or reducing false negatives (recall) are crucial, or where inference
time and computational efficiency have an impact on real-world deployment. Thus, implementing a complete
and consistent set of measures will guarantee that AI-based solutions are assessed in accordance with the
intricate requirements of actual agricultural contexts, in addition to improving comparability between
research.

interpretability in AI systems.
In healthcare, medical image analysis stands out as a domain where AI has been extensively applied, with
high accuracy complemented by improved model explainability and interpretability. Many studies in this area
focus on distinguishing between diseased and healthy tissues using visual tools such as saliency maps [77] which

6.Discussion
Figure 8 shows an bar chart illustrating the DL and XAI approaches employed in the summarized studies within
the agricultural domain. Among the DL models, ResNet exhibits the highest usage, followed by VGG. In the
XAI category, LIME is the most frequently employed method, followed by Grad-CAM, while SmoothGrad and
LightGBM are comparatively less utilized. Grad-CAM is widely used due to its versatility, as it can be applied to
image classification, object detection, and semantic segmentation tasks. Similarly, LIME’s model-agnostic
nature, along with its flexibility and adaptability, makes it a popular choice among XAI approaches compared
to others.
Figure 9 illustrates the classification of reviewed studies based on different agricultural task types. The
majority of agricultural tasks utilizing XAI approaches focus on plant and leaf disease classification, followed by
yield prediction, while vegetation mapping and plant phenotyping are the least explored. This trend can be
attributed to the abundance of image data, as large-scale datasets of diseased plant images and crop growth
patterns are readily available, facilitating the application of deep learning and XAI techniques. In contrast,
vegetation mapping and plant phenotyping involve complex environmental factors and large-scale spatial data,
making the integration of XAI techniques more challenging.
Stakeholder needs, data modality, and model type all influence how XAI techniques are adopted in agri-
culture: While feature significance and SHAP are typical for tabular yield models, visual attribution tools
(Grad-CAM, saliency) predominate in image-based disease/weed tasks, and new counterfactual and concept-
based approaches are becoming more and more respected for their applicability and conformity to agronomic
principles.
7.Challengesandlimitations
Creating a fully functional white box system that produces intelligible lay-person explanations with significant
user acceptability is XAI’s primary issue. This requires the collaboration of ML and XAI reserachers, as well as
user interface, user experience, usability, and human-computer interaction researchers. The goal of XAI is to
make decisions understandable and usable by human stakeholders who are not accustomed to technology.
Stakeholders of adoption, accessibility, and acceptance include domain experts, regulatory agencies, top

executives, and direct users of XAI. To achieve usability and acceptance, an amalgamation of XAI and
technology acceptance models should be used [82].
XAI enhances machine learning models, particularly those with abstract features. However, its effectiveness
depends on various factors like task, dataset, model architecture, explanation technique, and hyperparameters
[83]. Challenges include adaptive integration, consistent explanations, and interactive explanations [84]. To
ensure long term use and maintainability, resource management, algorithm efficiency optimization, and bal-
ancing predicted accuracy and model interpretability are crucial.
DL models trained on extensive plant phenotyping datasets often struggle with real world conditions,
where variables like fluctuating light and temperature are common. These models can be heavily influenced by
factors such as temperature variations, types of germination materials, pH levels of nutrient solutions, aeration,
lighting conditions, filter paper colors, and camera settings. Such biases make researchers question the general-
izability and reliability of deep learning models for plant studies. Relying on these models for assessing plant
varieties could lead to inaccurate results and suboptimal decision-making, posing potential risks for the future
of plant research [59].
Other challenges include:
• It is crucial but difficult to protect data privacy and security, integrate many data sources (geospatial, sensor,
and historical), and guarantee dependable connectivity [85].
• Changes in labour, knowledge-sharing, and benefit distribution brought about by XAI necessitate
cooperative prioritization and decision-making, which stakeholders must adjust to [86].
• To protect data integrity, it is essential to address cybersecurity, user permission, and data ownership [85].
• For XAI systems to be equitable, all farmers must have access to and be able to access them. It is difficult to
effectively communicate sophisticated AI results to a variety of stakeholders. Numerous researches have
documented the practical and computational issues that arise when balancing model simplicity and accuracy
in real-world applications [83, 85, 87–89].
• AI and XAI in agriculture face challenges like data availability and computational complexity, which may
hinder widespread adoption, especially in resource-limited regions [90].
8.Futuredirections
The integration of Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) into agricultural systems holds transformative
potential for enhancing the transparency, reliability, and ethical deployment of AI-enabled decision support
tools [91]. By addressing critical challenges related to interpretability, model accuracy, bias detection, and user

trust, XAI facilitates informed decision-making among key stakeholders, including farmers, agronomists,
researchers, and policymakers. Unlike conventional ‘black-box’ models, XAI frameworks provide human-
interpretable rationales for predictions or classifications, thereby promoting transparency and accountability
in data-driven agricultural interventions.
Moreover, the synergistic integration of XAI with emerging technologies such as blockchain, the Internet of
Things (IoT), and edge computing, which enables data processing directly on local devices like drones or farm
sensors rather than distant servers, hold promise for real-time agricultural applications. Edge computing
enhances the efficiency, security, and scalability of smart farming systems [92, 93]. For instance, XAI combined
with blockchain can enable tamper-proof data auditing and traceability in supply chain logistics, while XAI-
guided IoT deployments allow for real-time monitoring and autonomous decision- making with improved
interpretability at the edge. These integrated systems facilitate the deployment of precision agriculture prac-
tices, optimize resource utilization, and enhance food safety monitoring through traceable and interpretable
data analytics pipelines.
Despite these advancements, several technical and socio-economic challenges persist. The high main-
tenance costs of XAI-integrated systems, the absence of standardized automation protocols, and unresolved
ethical dilemmas associated with opaque or biased AI decision-making represent significant barriers to wide-
spread adoption [13, 94–96]. Additionally, the lack of user-friendly interfaces and limited domain-specific
interpretability tools impede the accessibility of XAI for non-expert end users, particularly in resource-con-
strained rural settings.
Future research should prioritize the development of sustainable, context-aware, and inclusive XAI frame-
works that uphold the principles of fairness, accountability, and transparency. This includes the design of light-
weight and explainable models suitable for deployment in low-power environments, the integration of multi-
modal sensor data for robust interpretability, and the incorporation of domain-specific knowledge into XAI
algorithms. Moreover, frameworks must be evaluated not only on performance metrics such as accuracy and
precision but also on their explainability, usability, and social acceptability among target user groups.
While Agriculture 5.0 envisions a highly interconnected, intelligent, and sustainable agricultural paradigm,
an over-dependence on automated technologies may inadvertently marginalize traditional farming knowl-
edge systems and impact rural employment. To ensure a balanced and ethical transition, future AI models must
incorporate principles of environmental sustainability, legal transparency, and human-centered governance.
This necessitates active collaboration between interdisciplinary stakeholders—including data scientists, ethi-
cists, legal experts, policymakers, and agricultural practitioners—to co-design responsible AI ecosystems tai-
lored to local socio-economic contexts [85], [97–102].
In summary, the responsible and strategic integration of XAI into agriculture presents a promising avenue
for building resilient, inclusive, and efficient agri-food systems. When aligned with ethical frameworks and
participatory development models, XAI can serve as a catalyst for equitable technological transformation in the
agricultural domain.
9.Conclusion
The agricultural sector is crucial for economic growth, employing significant human capital globally. Precision
agriculture helps address challenges like climate volatility, biological invasions, and resource scarcity. Ethical
concerns and regulatory mandates have sparked interest in model interpretability and explainability
frameworks, with research focusing on decentralized marketplace architectures, blockchain- enabled
provenance systems, and explainable AI implementations. XAI methodologies extend beyond weed
differentiation and disease diagnostics to predictive analytics for cultivar selection, optimizing resource
allocation, enhancing food security, and maximizing agricultural production. They integrate post-hoc
interpretability techniques like Grad-CAM, LIME, and SHAP, improving user interface accessibility,
algorithmic transparency, and stakeholder confidence among agricultural practitioners. The use of
interpretable artificial intelligence (XAI) in agriculture has shown significant progress, with tradi- tional
machine learning and deep learning architectures being the most commonly used. XAI-enabled precision
agriculture systems can reduce water consumption by 15%–25% while improving crop yields. However,
regional disparities exist, with developed economies implementing XAI more quickly than emerging markets.
The regulatory landscape for AI transparency in agriculture is evolving, emphasizing model auditability and
stakeholder accessibility. Implementation barriers include technical challenges in edge computing deployment
and underdeveloped knowledge transfer mechanisms for translating complex model explanations into
actionable insights. The review emphasizes the critical necessity for collaborative ecosystems encompassing
farmers, agricultural stakeholders, and computational scientists to facilitate the seamless integration of model-
agnostic interpretability techniques with established agricultural methodologies. Future research priorities
