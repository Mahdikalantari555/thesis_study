ABSTRACT
In recent years, deep learning has made significant advancements in the field of semantic segmentation of remote 
sensing images, owing to its exceptional feature extraction capabilities and robust modeling performance. 
However, due to the high dimensionality and multi-channel characteristics of time series remote sensing images, 
most current semantic segmentation algorithms still find it difficult to automatically and efficiently extract image 
features. Moreover, most models often overlook the integration of global and local information at multiple scales, 
especially for time series remote sensing images with large-scale variations. In this study, we propose a novel 
Bidirectional Temporal-Spatial vision Mamba-UNet (BTSM-UNet) for winter wheat mapping based on state space 
sequence models, which can efficiently capture complex dependencies in sequential data. This model follows an 
"Encoder + Decoder" structure, and a Bidirectional Temporal-Spatial vision Mamba (Bi-TSM) module is inserted 
to fully exploit spatiotemporal information from time series imagery. The results indicate that our BTSM-UNet 
achieved the best classification accuracy with an MCC of 0.905 and an F1-score of 0.952 for winter wheat 
mapping in the North China Plain. Additionally, the results of band combinations demonstrated that our pro-
posed model exhibited better stability compared to other deep models. Compared with the results of all band 
combinations, we found that the MCC (MCC/All) of BTSM-UNet decreased by only 2.43% when using RGB 
bands, while it decreased by 28.4% for the CNN-BiLSTM model, further proving the effectiveness and general-
ization ability of our method. Moreover, the BTSM-UNet exhibited the fastest data processing rate (24 FPS), a 
relatively small number of model parameters (4.86 M), and low computational complexity (60.19 G) compared 
to other models. We expect that the proposed method will provide a practical and effective technical approach 
for accurately and intelligently mapping winter wheat fields.
1. Introduction
Winter wheat is one of the important staple crops in the world, 
effective mapping of winter wheat is crucial for optimizing crop struc-
tures, assessing crop productivity, and monitoring crop health [4,35]. 
Due to its ability to acquire timely data, provide wide coverage, and 
operate at a low cost, remote sensing technology has emerged as an 
increasingly powerful tool for mapping winter wheat spatially [13,31]. 
Currently, winter wheat classification methods based on remote sensing 
are mainly divided into two categories: single image-based methods and 
time series image-based methods. However, due to the influence of 
temporal resolution and weather conditions, it is often difficult to obtain 
the key phenological period images of winter wheat based on a single 
image. Due to the ability of time-series remote sensing images to provide 
information on changes in time, spectrum, and texture at different points 
during the winter wheat growing season, they can better capture the 
phenological rhythm characteristics of winter wheat. Given the wealth 
of temporal and spectral information, time-series remote sensing imag-
ery has become an important data source in the field of crop mapping [6,
11].

series images characterized by varying spatial resolutions and

over large areas. Sentinel satellite remote sensing images have seen 
increasing application in the extraction of winter wheat distribution 
information over recent years, driven by their superior spectral, spatial, 
and temporal resolutions compared to alternative datasets [13,16,17]. 
With a 5-day revisit frequency, spatial resolution as fine as 10 m, and the 
addition of three red-edge spectral bands, Sentinel-2 has shown great 
promise for winter wheat mapping.
Considering the unique spectral features of different crops in 
different growing periods, most studies acquire the spatial distribution 
of winter wheat using temporal features and machine learning classifiers 
[2,28]. However, approaches emphasizing temporal features do not 
consider the spatial variability between objects, a factor that is essential 
for accurate mapping of winter wheat. Moreover, geographical factors 
lead to significant intra- and inter-class variations in winter wheat 
phenology, resulting in poor spatial and temporal transferability for 
methods relying on temporal features [22]. Machine learning classifiers, 
such as Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, and Decision Trees, 
have mitigated the problem and offered effective methods for extracting 
spatial features. However, these feature extractors were heavily reliant 
on domain-specific knowledge and failed to effectively capture the 
sequential aspects of the winter wheat life cycle [20].
During the past several years, deep learning has emerged as a 
powerful tool with superior learning and feature extraction capabilities, 
showing great potential in remote sensing applications [6,12,32]. Dur-
ing this time, a proliferation of deep neural networks (DNNs) has 
emerged, significantly influencing conventional time-series approaches 
to winter wheat mapping. Convolutional networks and recurrent net-
works are currently popular deep-learning architectures for time-series 
crop classification [7,37]. Temporal networks that incorporate convo-
lutions in both time and spectrum dimensions are capable of thoroughly 
exploring spatial and temporal details in time-series datasets. To meet 
the demands of various downstream tasks in the field of crop segmen-
tation, the research based on time-series images focuses on constructing 
a network structure that efficiently integrates spatiotemporal features 
across different time periods [18,36]. However, most convolution 
models fail to consider the sequential aspects of time series, which im-
pairs their performance in learning long-term dependencies. Although 
the recurrent networks are capable of detecting time dependencies and 
evolving features in sequential data, they tend to experience gradient

2. Material and methods
2.1. Study area
In this study, we chose ten sites within China’s primary wheat- 
producing regions to carry out our experiments (Fig. 1). Eight sites (A 
to H) were designated for training, while the remaining two (T1 and T2) 
were used for testing. Each site encompasses a rectangular area of 
approximately 4000 × 4000 pixels, equivalent to 40,000 × 40,000 
square meters. The distribution of these sites covers important produc-
tion areas for winter wheat in China, with variations in geographical 
environment, climatic conditions, phenological characteristics, and soil 
conditions across different regions. Such site selection allows for a 
comprehensive consideration of spatial and spectral differences of 
winter wheat objects in images from different regions, ensuring the 
generalization and representativeness of the training samples. These ten 
sites fall within the temperate monsoon climate and subtropical 
monsoon climate zones. Winters are dry and cold, while summers are 
hot with abundant rainfall, and springs tend to be dry with little rain. 
The region is well-endowed with heat resources, which support a variety 
of double-cropping systems. The accumulated temperature above 0 ◦C 
ranges from 4100 to 5400 ◦C, and the accumulated temperature above 
10 ◦C ranges from 3700 to 4700 ◦C, allowing different types of winter 
wheat to safely overwinter. The frost-free period lasts from 190 to 220 
days, and the annual precipitation on the plain ranges from 500 to 1000 
mm . The predominant planting patterns were winter wheat and sum-
mer maize, with winter wheat serving as the main crop harvested in

2.2. Sentinel-2 imagery
In this study, we used multi-temporal Sentinel-2 surface reflectance 
products to perform the winter wheat classification. The Sentinel-2 
constellation comprises two satellites, each outfitted with a Multispec-
tral Instrument (MSI) that covers 13 bands from visible to short-wave 
infrared wavelengths, offering spatial resolutions between 10 and 60 
m Featuring a 290 km wide swath and a 10-day revisit interval per 
satellite, the system can achieve a revisit cycle of every 5 days, making it 
an ideal data source for monitoring and mapping crops. To avoid 
redundant preprocessing, preprocessed Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface 
reflectance images were downloaded directly from Google Earth Engine 
(GEE). While terrain correction and cloud masking are typically 
required, terrain correction was omitted due to topographic homoge-
neity across the North China Plain study area. To eliminate cloud 
interference, all images acquired during the winter wheat growing 
season (1 October 2022 - 30 June 2023) were filtered by a < 20 % cloud 
cover threshold. Ultimately, 152 clear-sky time-series images spanning 
ten experimental stations were acquired for subsequent experiments. To 
maintain temporal information throughout the growing season, we kept 
all observations along with their corresponding Day of Year (DoY), 
which helps capture specific phenological events of winter wheat. To 
achieve winter wheat classification, we selected ten spectral bands, 
excluding the ones with a 60 m spatial resolution and Band 8A because 
of its spectral overlap with Band 8 (Table 1).
2.3. Winter wheat reference samples
We used the winter wheat distribution datasets developed by Yang 
et al. [38] as the reference source of ground-truth information for winter 
wheat. Using multi-source imagery and extensive ground truth data, the 
products were developed with a spatial resolution of 10 m. Based on a 
knowledge-based approach and machine learning classifiers, this pub-
lication extracted accurate distribution data of winter wheat from 2020 
to 2022. To assess the quality of the training data generated, the trained 
classification model was applied across various years. The evaluation 
revealed that integrating the generated training data with a machine 
learning classifier led to the highest accuracy across all classification 
scenarios. The products consistently achieved high accuracy, with 
overall accuracies above 0.90 from 2020 to 2022. Moreover, the esti-
mated areas correlated strongly with agricultural census data, achieving 
R² values of at least 0.95 at the provincial level and 0.91 at the municipal 
level.

3. Methodology
3.1. Background
Mamba is a State Space Model (SSM) architecture and is one of the 
most successful variants of SSMs. Mamba introduces a simple but highly 
effective selection mechanism that parametrizes the SSM based on the 
input, allowing the model to retain relevant data indefinitely while 
filtering out irrelevant information [42]. Additionally, Mamba includes 
a hardware-aware algorithm that uses scan operations instead of con-
volutions to compute the model iteratively, which can speed up com-
putations by three times on an A100 GPU. With its strong capability to 
model long sequences and near-linear scalability, Mamba has become a 
foundational model.
As a key module of Vision Mamba, the Mamba block utilizes position 
embeddings to encode image sequences and employs a bidirectional 
state space model to compress visual representations, enabling Vision 
Mamba to effectively capture the global context of images. This 
approach addresses the inherent position sensitivity of visual data, a 
critical issue often encountered by traditional Transformer models. In 
particular, Vision Mamba outperforms traditional Transformers (such as 
Vision Transformer) in terms of performance and memory efficiency 
when handling high-resolution data tasks. Unlike many models that 
process data unidirectionally, Vision Mamba’s encoder processes tokens 
in both forward and backward directions, allowing for a richer under-
standing of image context in accurate image classification.
3.2. BTSM-UNet
The proposed Bidirectional Temporal-Spatial vision Mamba-UNet 
(BTSM-UNet) generally follows an "Encoder + Decoder" structure 
(Fig. 2). In the middle three layers of the Encoder, we inserted the 
Bidirectional Temporal-Spatial vision Mamba (Bi-TSM) structure to fully 
exploit spatiotemporal information at different scales. In the Decoder 
part, by combining local spatial features captured by the CNN and global 
spatiotemporal features captured by the Bi-TSM module at various 
scales, we achieved enhanced accuracy in wheat extraction. First, we 
used multiple cascaded 2D CNN blocks to extract multi-scale spatial 
detail features from the input time-series images. Second, we applied the 
Bi-TSM module to the three middle feature maps to further mine their 
spatiotemporal information, compensating for the loss of temporal in-
formation in the 2D CNN architecture. Then, we fused the spatial detail 
information obtained in the first step with the global spatiotemporal 
information obtained in the second step using deconvolution operations, 
increasing the resolution of the feature maps. Finally, we applied a 
single deconvolution layer to produce a classification output that 
matches the resolution of the input image.
3.2.1. Theoretical basis of temporal-spatial decoupling
The core innovation of our proposed Bidirectional Temporal-Spatial 
Decoupled Mamba (Bi-TSM) module is the decomposition of complex 
spatio-temporal modeling into two sequential stages: spatial modeling 
followed by temporal modeling. This section provides a theoretical 
justification for this decoupling strategy over traditional joint processing 
methods, focusing on computational complexity and information 
retention capabilities. 
(1) Analysis of computational complexity
Joint models (e.g., 3D-CNNs) process all dimensions of the data in a 
unified operation, leading to computational complexity that scales 
exponentially with the input size. For an input tensor with a batch size of 
1, temporal length T, spatial dimensions H × W, and input/output 
channels Cin and Cout, the computational complexity of a basic 3D 
convolution kernel (of size t × k × k) is approximately: 
Ojoint ≈ T⋅H⋅W⋅Cin⋅Cout ⋅t⋅k2 (1) 
This represents a multiplicative relationship between the temporal 
and spatial dimensions. When processing large-scale (large H, W) and 
long-term (large T) remote sensing data, this leads to a significant 
computational burden, often referred to as the "curse of dimensionality."
In contrast, our Bi-TSM module employs a decoupled strategy. First, 
the spatial modeling stage (e.g., using 2D-CNNs) has a complexity of: 
Ospace ≈ H⋅W⋅Cin ⋅Cout ⋅k2 (2) 
Subsequently, the temporal modeling stage processes the feature 
sequence of length T and dimension D using the Bidirectional Visual 
Mamba. Benefiting from the linear time-invariant system properties and 
the selective scanning mechanism of State Space Models (SSMs), the 
complexity of this stage is optimized to approximately O(T), which is 
significantly lower than the O(T2) complexity of Transformer-based 
attention mechanisms. Thus, the overall complexity of our decoupled 
approach is: 
Odecoupled ≈ Ospace+ O(T⋅D) (3) 
This demonstrates that our method successfully reduces the 
complexity from a multiplicative scale in joint methods to an additive 
scale. This leads to markedly improved computational efficiency when 
handling large-scale remote sensing time series, without compromising 
model performance. 
(2) Advantages in information retention and feature learning
Joint models, which fuse spatio-temporal information in a single 
operation, can lead to learned feature representations that suffer from 
"spatio-temporal entanglement." For the winter wheat identification 
task, this means the model may conflate spatial structure information (e. 
g., field shape, texture) with the critically important phenological tem-
poral information (e.g., spectral changes during green-up and heading 
stages). Given that existing literature strongly suggests that temporal

3.2.3. Multi-scale bidirectional temporal-spatial vision mamba (Bi-TSM)
To account for the implicit merging of the time dimension T when 
shifting from 3D CNN to 2D CNN, we incorporated a Bi-TSM module at 
the feature map scales of 128, 64, and 32. This structure leverages the 
strong modeling capabilities of Bi-TSM for temporal information to fully 
exploit spatiotemporal global information, thereby compensating for the 
loss of temporal information in 2D CNNs. The Bi-TSM is a decoupled 
Vision Mamba structure designed with a serial configuration of 
Temporal-Vision Mamba (Temporal-VM) and Spatial-Vision Mamba 
(Spatial-VM).
Fig. 3
(1) Tokenization of Bi-TSM
To better capture the local features and reduce information loss, we 
performed conv2d on feature maps at resolutions of 128 × 128, 64 × 64, 
and 32 × 32 to obtain feature embeddings instead of patch splicing at 
different scales. The convolution kernel sizes are 8 × 8, 4 × 4, and 2 × 2, 
with corresponding strides of 8, 4, and 2, respectively. Like patch 
processing, we obtained N non-overlapping feature blocks after the 
convolution operation. Then, we applied linear transformation on each 
feature block to get embedding vectors of specific dimensions. Consid-
ering the effect of scale changes, we set the dimensions of the embedding 
vectors to 128, 256, and 512 respectively. To adequately model the 
spatial and temporal context information, T time embeddings were 
explicitly added to the temporal embedding vector, while N position 
embeddings were explicitly added to the spatial embedding vector. 
(2) Temporal-VM

with a stride of (t, h, w) across the temporal and spatial dimensions. This 
process yields N = [T/t][H/h][W/w] tokens, each denoted as Xi ∈ RthwC,

We employ 2D deconvolution for the Decoder part of the convolution 
block. Following five deconvolution modules, the feature map resolu-
tions gradually increased to 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128. Each module con-

ReLU activation layer. The feature maps were fused with the corre-
sponding feature maps obtained from the Bi-TSM at the 32, 64, and 128 
scales before the deconvolution operation.

5.1. Performance of the BTSM-UNet
We trained the BTSM-UNet on eight training sites and transferred it 
to the two testing sites. The classification results shown in Table 2
indicate that the predictions from BTSM-UNet exhibit excellent spatial 
consistency with the ground truth samples. Although there is some

respectively (Table 2).
In addition to quantitative evaluation, qualitative analysis was also 
employed to evaluate the performance of our BTSM-UNet (Fig. 4). The 
visual results of the predicted results showed high spatial accuracy for 
identifying wheat pixels (Fig. 4). The zoomed-in images provided a 
closer look at parcel details and highlighted the differences between 
predictions and actual wheat areas, demonstrating high accuracy in 
identifying wheat locations. Additionally, the BTSM-UNet model

of misidentified patches, the overall identification result was highly 
consistent with the ground truth data (Fig. 4, Site T2, A, a).

5.2. Comparative experiments with other deep learning models
In our study, we compared our proposed model with six mainstream 
deep-learning models (Table 3). Among the evaluated models, BTSM- 
UNet achieved the highest classification accuracy in identifying winter 
wheat, with an MCC of 0.905 and an F1-score of 0.952. The UNet3d 
model exhibited slightly lower classification performance compared to 
BTSM-UNet, achieving an MCC of 0.882 and an F1-score of 0.929. 
Additionally, UTS-Former outperformed TSViT but was slightly weaker 
than BTSM-UNet, achieving an MCC of 0.881 and an F1-score of 0.936. 
Compared to BTSM-UNet, UTS-Former’s MCC decreased by 2.65 %, and 
its F1-score decreased by 1.86 %. The classification accuracy of VM- 
UNet was slightly lower than that of UTS-Former, with an MCC of 
0.843 and an F1-score of 0.925. The CNN-BiLSTM model had the poorest 
classification performance, obtaining an MCC of 0.659 and an F1-score 
of 0.813, which is approximately 27.2 % lower in MCC compared to 
BTSM-UNet.
The spatial prediction results based on different deep learning 
models indicate that BTSM-UNet provides superior visual segmentation 
results and accurate identification of plot boundaries at both testing sites 
compared to several current mainstream deep learning models (Fig. 5). 
From the segmentation results, it is obvious that BTSM-UNet effectively 
extracts wheat plots while ensuring their integrity. Additionally, there is 
minimal salt-and-pepper noise within the segmented wheat plots, 
accurately depicting the spatial distribution characteristics of wheat. For 
non-wheat categories, BTSM-UNet successfully excludes wheat pixels 
and delineates other land cover types such as residential areas, roads, 
and rivers, preserving their complete boundaries and detailed 
information. The segmented non-wheat patches also exhibit very little 
salt-and-pepper noise or trace of image clipping. In contrast, other deep 
learning models like UNet3d, although capable of accurately identifying 
wheat plots and their boundaries, still have room for improvement in 
capturing plot details and smaller plots. The VM-UNet clearly mis-
identified the small paths between the plot boundaries as wheat, thereby 
reducing the classification accuracy of the model (Fig. 6g). While UTS- 
Former achieves good segmentation accuracy, it exhibits noticeable 
salt-and-pepper noise within the segmented plots compared to BTSM- 
UNet (Fig. 5f, Fig. 6f).
We also compared the number of parameters, computational 
complexity, and inference speed of different deep models (Table 4). The 
results demonstrated that our BTSM-UNet achieved the highest pro-

(12.77 M), and exhibited low computational complexity (6.11 GFLOPs) 
at an input resolution of 64 × 64 (Table 4). In contrast, the UTS-Former,

number of parameters and computational complexity, its inference 
speed is lower.

6. Discussion
6.1. Effects of feature combinations and temporal resolution
This study involved the training and testing of models utilizing 
varying temporal sequence lengths across ten distinct locations. The 
results demonstrated excellent performance in winter wheat classifica-
tion across all sites. Nevertheless, significant differences in classification 
performance were observed among the different temporal lengths, 
indicating that the duration of the time series considerably influences 
model performance. A comparative experiment was conducted at the 
site T2 with the longest available time series to systematically evaluate 
the effect of image time series length on classification performance 
(Supplementary Materials). The experimental results indicate that the 
model’s classification accuracy exhibits an upward trend with an in-
crease in the number of temporal images (Fig. 10). The Matthews Cor-
relation Coefficient (MCC) values for the six combinations ranged from 
0.20 to 0.80, whereas the F1-score values ranged from 0.70 to 0.90. The 
lower MCC values relative to the F1-scores are primarily due to the 
imbalanced distribution of positive and negative samples in the dataset, 
given that the MCC calculation incorporates the distributions of all 
samples (both positive and negative), while the F1-score primarily fo-
cuses on the positive class.
To further identify the most discriminative image acquisition periods 
for winter wheat classification, we selected the highest-accuracy results 
from each experimental set and calculated the frequency of each 
acquisition date across all combinations. A higher frequency of a specific 
acquisition date among high-accuracy results indicates its greater 
contribution to correct classification, highlighting its stronger discrim-
inative power. Analysis revealed that images from the early and late 
growth stages of winter wheat appeared most frequently in high- 
accuracy combinations, suggesting that imagery from these phenolog-
ical phases is particularly critical for successful classification. In the 
highest-accuracy combinations, images acquired during the harvesting 
period (late growth stage) exhibited the highest frequency, followed by 
those from the emergence and wintering periods (early growth stage) 
(Table 8).

Mapping (Grad-CAM) technique to visualize features within the tem-

6.2. Explanation of the model advantages
In this study, we proposed a Bidirectional Temporal-Spatial Vision 
Mamba-UNet on time-series imagery and tested its performance in both 
the northern and southern areas across China. Both test sites achieved 
high accuracy; for example, the predicted precision of winter wheat in 
sites T1 and T2 are 0.95 and 0.96, respectively. The accuracy difference 
between the two sites can mainly be attributed to the length of the time- 
series sequence, with site T1 having 22 acquisition dates and site T2 
having 32 acquisition dates. The longer time-series sequence retains 
more temporal signals and better models the changing relationship 
during the winter wheat growing season, especially for images at key 
phenological stages [5]. Moreover, our method can better capture 
temporal dependencies through a bidirectional modeling mechanism, 
effectively simulating the growth patterns of winter wheat over time. In 
addition, the visual mapping of the predicted results also showed 
satisfactory performance, with wheat and non-wheat pixels well 
distinguished and field boundaries well extracted using the BTSM-UNet 
model. This result benefits from the ability of Vision Mamba to effec-
tively capture the global context of images, addressing the position 
sensitivity inherent in visual data, especially for high-resolution imagery 
[40,42].
This study used 9 Sentinel-2 bands for model training and testing, 
including three visible light bands (Red, Green, and Blue), one near- 
infrared (NIR) band, three red-edge bands, and two short-wave 
infrared bands. We found that the quantitative accuracy of different 
band combinations was generally similar for winter wheat identifica-
tion. This is likely because the phenological changes in winter wheat are 
more significant than those in other land cover types, making it easier 
for the temporal model to identify winter wheat signals in multiple 
bands [21,26]. Notably, the inclusion of red-edge bands contributed 
more significantly to improving classification accuracy compared to the 
addition of short-wave infrared bands (Table 5). This may be because 
red-edge bands are typically more sensitive to biochemical content in

The proposed method in this paper achieves better results than 
existing mainstream deep models in extracting features from multi- 
temporal images and optimizing model runtime efficiency. Our statis-
tical results show that the method proposed in this paper has significant 
improvements over the current mainstream several time series models 
(Fig. 12). The four accuracy indicators of the BTSM model (i.e., MCC, F1- 
score, Precision, and Recall) are almost all significantly higher than 
those of the other six mainstream models. For instance, the MCC of the 
BTSM model is approximately 28 % higher than that of the CNN-BiLSTM 
model, and the F1 score is nearly 15 % higher. Our significance analysis 
also provides a quantitative demonstration of the superiority of this 
method in terms of extraction accuracy. UNet3d and UTS-Former 
(embedded with the Transformer framework) have demonstrated 
excellent segmentation performance in remote sensing research, but 
these networks primarily rely on multiple convolutional layers or 
transformers to extract and fuse local and global feature information, 
leading to high model complexity and low runtime efficiency [4,7]. In 
contrast, our method can reduce the model parameter count to 4.86 M 
and the computational cost to 60 GFLOPs, while achieving a runtime 
efficiency of 24FPS under the same input data resolution conditions. 
UNet3d has a computational cost of 2159 GFLOPs and a runtime effi-
ciency of 9 FPS (Table 4). UTS-Former uses a spatiotemporal feature 
extraction module to bridge the encoder and decoder of the network, 
effectively compensating for the loss of temporal features caused by 2D 
convolutions. However, the vision transformer used in UTS-Former has a 
high model complexity that scales quadratically with sequence length, 
limiting its application in high-resolution, long-time series data [4]. In 
comparison, the Bidirectional Vision Mamba model inherits the char-
acteristics of state-space models, enabling it to process long sequences in 
linear time, making it ideally suited for dealing with high-resolution, 
long-term remote sensing data series [15]. The complexity of our 
method is 50 % lower than that of UTS-Former, and the data processing 
speed is three times faster. The goal of an effective model is not to 
maximize its parameter count but to achieve high parameter efficiency 
and information utility, effectively acting as an adaptive filter that dis-
cards noise while preserving task-relevant signals. The selective mech-
anism inherent in the State Space Model (e.g., Mamba) fulfills this role. 
It dynamically prioritizes critical phenological stages (e.g., heading and 
maturing) while suppressing irrelevant temporal information (e.g., 
dormant phases), thereby allocating limited computational resources to 
the most discriminative features. This selective compression aligns with 
the information bottleneck principle, which favors minimal sufficient 
representations. Consequently, the model achieves an optimal balance 
between representational power and complexity. This explains why a 
reduction in parameters and FLOPs does not compromise performance 
but instead leads to a more efficient and generalizable architecture.
6.3. Limitations and potential applications
Although the proposed method achieved promising results in this 
study, it faces certain challenges when applied to other large-scale 
winter wheat remote sensing identification. Specifically, more 
research is needed to evaluate how well the model generalizes to other 
regions with varying crop planting structures and phenological condi-
tions. The model was trained and validated using large, relatively flat 
plots in the North China Plain (NCP). Its application in mountainous 
areas poses significant challenges not explicitly addressed in the current 
study. Factors such as phenological shifts, varied geographical envi-
ronments, diverse farming practices, and data quality issues all 
significantly impact the model’s performance. Nevertheless, experi-
mental results from fragmented plots in Xinyang and Huainan, which 
are characterized by a wheat-rice rotation in contrast to the wheat-corn 
rotation dominant in the NCP, demonstrate that the resulting spatial 
distribution maps capture winter wheat plot boundaries more compre-
hensively (Supplementary Materials, Fig. 13). Although some misclas-
sification and omission errors occurred in fragmented areas, the overall 
performance remained satisfactory, with Matthews Correlation Coeffi-
cient (MCC) values of 0.87 in Xinyang and 0.83 in Huainan. Moving 
forward, integrating digital elevation models (DEMs) for topographic 
correction or developing terrain-invariant feature representations will 
be essential for achieving robust performance in such complex regions. 
In addition, the success of our phenology-based model hinges on the 
target crop possessing a clear and unique temporal signature. Winter 
wheat, with its characteristic green-up in early spring followed by rapid 
senescence, is an ideal case in point. Extending this approach to crops 
like corn or soybean could be challenging, as they often have over-
lapping growing seasons and less spectrally distinct phenological phases 
in intensive agricultural systems. The model would require retraining 
and might achieve lower accuracy if the interspecies temporal separa-
bility is reduced. Future work should explicitly test the model’s trans-
ferability across a wider range of agro-ecosystems.
Future research should also delve deeper into enhancing the model’s 
generalization capabilities. This entails enhancing the model’s adapt-
ability to diverse regional geographical environments, phenological 
changes, and climatic conditions, as well as its compatibility with data 
from various sensors [41]. A promising avenue for all-weather crop 
monitoring is the integration of SAR data from sensors like Sentinel-1. 
However, fusing optical and SAR time series presents a considerable 
challenge. The physiologically meaningful optical indices (e.g., NDVI) 
and the structurally sensitive SAR backscatter represent fundamentally 
different physical quantities. Developing a unified framework that 
effectively leverages the complementary strengths of both data type-
s—using SSMs to model their complex, coupled temporal dynam-
ics—remains an open and highly valuable research challenge [30]. 
Another critical consideration for the practical application of crop 
mapping methods is their robustness to data noise and occlusion, 
particularly from cloud cover. While this study intentionally controlled 
cloud cover (all images <20 %) to isolate the core objective of evalu-
ating phenological sequence construction, the impact of cloud contam-
ination warrants discussion. It is important to note that the impact of 
cloud cover is not solely a function of its spatial coverage but is intrin-
sically coupled with the phenological timing of the obscured observa-
tion. Our model’s performance likely exhibits a nonlinear sensitivity to 
cloudy images depending on the growth stage they occlude. For 
instance, the loss of a clear observation during a transitional or 
non-critical period may have a minimal impact due to the inherent 
redundancy in the temporal sequence. In contrast, the occlusion of a key 
phenological window (e.g., the highly discriminative green-up or 
heading stages) could significantly degrade accuracy, as the model 
would be deprived of the most salient spectral-temporal features. This 
coupling effect makes a simplistic evaluation based solely on uniform 
cloud coverage percentages less meaningful. Therefore, a more nuanced 
future investigation is essential, one that systematically analyzes the 
interaction between the timing of data gaps/noise and model perfor-
mance, rather than just the magnitude of coverage. This would provide a 
truly comprehensive assessment of robustness for operational deploy-
ment. Furthermore, several advanced Mamba variants, such as 
RS-Mamba [40] and Samba [43], have recently demonstrated remark-
able performance in single-temporal remote sensing image analysis. 
While a direct comparison was not feasible within our multi-temporal 
framework, their innovative designs (e.g., specialized remote sensing 
adaptations, efficient skip-connections) offer valuable inspiration. A 
promising future direction is to explore how these architectures can be 
extended or integrated into temporal modeling paradigms, potentially 
leading to even more powerful spatio-temporal analyzers for

7. Conclusions
In this study, we introduced a novel Bidirectional Temporal-Spatial 
Vision Mamba-UNet (BTSM-UNet) for Sentinel-2 imagery to enhance 
the efficiency and segmentation accuracy of winter wheat mapping. We 
designed an innovative spatiotemporal decoupled temporal feature 
extraction module based on the bidirectional vision mamba structure 
(Bi-TSM) which can better extract spatiotemporal global information 
and improve the processing efficiency of long-series images. By using 
multi-scale fusion techniques, the Bi-TSM is applied to different feature 
map scales to reduce the loss of temporal context information when 
processing 3D time-series data. The results demonstrated that our BTSM- 
UNet outperformed mainstream deep models, achieving average MCC 
and F1 scores of 0.905 and 0.952, respectively. Additionally, multi-band 
combinations showed superior performance in terms of accuracy and 
model stability. Moreover, our BTSM-UNet achieved significant 
improvement in data processing efficiency, a decrease in the number of 
parameters, and a reduction in model complexity, demonstrating the 
high efficiency and good performance of our proposed method. To our 
knowledge, this represents the first effort to achieve high-quality wheat 
mapping based on multi-temporal imagery by leveraging Mamba’s state 
space sequence models. Furthermore, the modular architecture of our 
model facilitates seamless adaptation to multi-source remote sensing 
data (such as Landsat) and diverse agroecological zones, establishing a 
technical reference for high-precision crop identification across het-
erogeneous landscapes.
