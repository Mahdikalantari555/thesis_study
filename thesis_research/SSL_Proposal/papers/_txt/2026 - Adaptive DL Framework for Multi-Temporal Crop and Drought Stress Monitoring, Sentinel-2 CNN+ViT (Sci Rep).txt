Abstract
Precision agriculture increasingly relies on data-driven methods to address the challenges of crop stress and 
drought monitoring under changing climatic conditions. Hence, a deep learning (DL) based innovative frame -
work is required for assessing crop stress dynamics, aimed at improving agricultural productivity and ensur -
ing regional food security, because conventional models often struggle to capture subtle spatial and spectral 
variations, resulting in limited accuracy and generalizability. To overcome these challenges, an innovative and 
adaptive DL-based approach has been proposed to utilize multi-temporal Sentinel-2 satellite images collected 
over Haldharmau village, Gonda District, Uttar Pradesh. The model effectively integrates local spectral and 
spatial details with broader contextual features, enabling crop classification and stress detection to support pre -
cision agriculture. The crop stress and drought maps have been generated with the help of a CNN+ViT model 
(i.e., A hybrid DL-based classification model) based classified images in conjunction with NDVI and NDWI 
images to evaluate drought-induced crop variability across 2023, 2024, and 2025. The monthly (January to 
June) stress maps derived from this process provide valuable insights into temporal patterns of crop exposure. 
The proposed framework offers quantitative insights into temporal and spatial patterns of crop vulnerability 
and resilience across semi‐arid agricultural landscapes. Therefore, the proposed framework advances precision 
agriculture modeling by integrating satellite images, deep learning, and phenological analysis, and is readily 
transferable to other drought-prone regions with analogous crop systems.

Introduction
Consistent monitoring of crop stress conditions is made possible through digital image processing by utiliz -
ing satellite images, which can identify minute physiological changes before they manifest as symptoms. By 
using the multispectral characteristics from optical satellite images, machine learning (ML) algorithms identify 
and forecast stress levels, enhance classification accuracy, and minimize reliance on manual calibration 1,2. This

data-driven, scalable solution provides predictive insights, ongoing monitoring, and early detection. The ability 
to evaluate stress over vast agricultural landscapes has improved with the integration of digital image processing 
techniques and satellite images. Common techniques include visual scoring, biomass sampling, principal compo-
nent analysis (PCA), thermal imaging, chlorophyll fluorescence and spectroradiometry, and vegetation indicators 
(VIs)3. Nevertheless, these techniques have drawbacks such as delayed detection, subjectivity in visual evalua -
tions, significant reliance on field access, and restricted spatial and temporal coverage. Subjectivity, spatial cover-
age, and delayed detection are the drawbacks of conventional techniques for identifying abiotic stress in crops. 
They are not feasible for extensive surveillance since they depend on observable symptoms and time-consuming 
fieldwork4. Additionally, because stress is typically detected only after outward symptoms have materialized, 
often too late for effective intervention, they tend to be reactive rather than proactive. Although point-based 
instruments, such as spectroradiometers or chlorophyll meters, offer precise data, their spatial representativeness 
is constrained, and they are unable to capture the diversity between areas or within entire fields5. Significant noise 
and variability in the results can also be introduced by human error, crop variability, and environmental factors. 
In contemporary PA systems, these constraints together impede prompt decision-making, early stress detection, 
and resource-efficient crop management by utilizing satellite images efficiently and effectively.
Various studies have been conducted for land cover classification or rainfall forecasting in the Indo-Gangetic 
areas or eastern parts of Uttar Pradesh state; however, systematic assessments of crop and drought stress using 
satellite images remain limited and still a very challenging task in these regions 6,7. Hence, a satellite image-
based approach is required to monitor crop and drought stress regularly, which can enable early identification 
of stress-prone areas, supporting timely intervention and sustainable fruit crop management. In a brief literature 
review, it is observed that Sentinel-2 satellite images are capable of assessing abiotic stress in crops, such as 
drought, nutrient deficiency, or heat stress. In comparison to the Sentinel-2 images, Landsat series images have 
a comparatively longer revisit time and cloud contamination issues and a coarser spatial resolution of 30 m, 
whereas MODIS has a high temporal resolution with a revisit frequency of approximately 1–2 days but it is 
limited by its very low spatial resolution, with a minimum resolution of 250, which makes them less suitable for 
detailed spatial level stress monitoring for precision agiculture applications 8. Therefore, since 2015, Sentinel-2 
has played a pivotal role in the assessment of crop stress due to its high spatial resolution (i.e., 10 m) and 5-day 
revisit time, which provide detailed and frequent monitoring of agricultural areas. Various indices like normalized 
difference vegetation index (NDVI), normalized difference water index (NDWI), soil adjusted vegetation index 
(SA VI), green normalized vegetation difference index (GNDVI), and the pigment-specific simple ratio (PSSR) 
obtained through Sentinel-2 satellite images have been utilized to identify the stress conditions over large agri -
cultural areas8–11.
ML and DL techniques, such as support vector machine (SVM), random forests (RF), and convolutional neural 
networks (CNNs), can further enhance stress assessment by modeling complex relationships between spectral 
features and field conditions12,13. The effectiveness of abiotic stress detection depends on precise pre-processing 
steps, and the Sentinel-2 images are quite useful for large-scale monitoring of crop stress by capturing detailed 
spectral variations associated with crop health and stress levels. DL models have been effectively implemented 
in greenhouses to forecast crop stress by using physiological and environmental data. Crop identification has 
improved because of DL models like convolutional neural networks and imaging-based techniques. Addition -
ally, ML and DL models can identify crop diseases early on, assisting farmers in preventing them and increasing 
yields12,14,15. Spectral indices are used to analyze crop health by analyzing the interaction between electromag -
netic radiation and foliage16. Influences include photosynthetic pigments, water content, and air spaces. Vegetation 
indices can monitor plant-health issues like fungal pathogens, excess salt, and nutrient deficiencies. Fluorescence 
of chlorophyll measures photosynthetic efficiency and productivity but is limited for remote sensing. A standard 
Photochemical Reflectance Index (PRI) measures photosynthetic light use efficiency, but comparison with tra -
ditional spectrometric equipment is crucial. Satellite-based spectral indices can also be used to measure nutrient 
stress in plants, assessing deficiencies and heavy metal contamination. It can be used to identify nutrient-poor

In recent years, many researchers have explored ML-based methods for the assessment of abiotic stress by 
using the commonly used traditional ML supervised algorithms like SVM, neural networks (NN), and maximum 
likelihood estimation (MLE), and unsupervised algorithms like K-Means clustering for satellite image classi -
fication due to their simplicity, interpretability, and low processing requirements, while RF has been used for 
identifying crop stress18,19. These algorithms are effective on short datasets but struggle with high-dimensional 
data, mixed pixels, and non-linear class borders, making them ineffective in complex agricultural surroundings. 
Supervised classification approaches use labeled training data and perform well with reliable ground truth data. 
However, SVM is sensitive to kernel and parameter adjustments and can become computationally rigorous with 
large datasets. DL-based models like CNNs are the most effective for image-based classification and detection 
of crop stress and drought stress20. Their hierarchical feature learning provides better contextual comprehension 
and spatial accuracy than conventional classifiers like SVM and K-Means. However, CNNs have limitations, 
such as noticeable salt-and-pepper noise in classified images, which can lead to misclassifications or uncertainty 
in boundaries. To address these issues, a U-Net-based semantic segmentation model was proposed, which offers 
pixel-wise classification, allowing for full-resolution, spatially coherent, categorized maps 21. DL-based models 
like U-Net and Vision Transformer (ViT) have also been used for detecting crop stress due to their deep feature 
extraction capabilities. These methods have significantly increased the precision and effectiveness of determin -
ing plant health conditions, ultimately improving crop management and stress prevention tactics 22. These mod-
els, with their encoder-decoder design and skip connections, maintain spatial context and are more suitable for 
accurate mapping and analysis of crop stress. However, U-Net has limitations, such as over-smoothing class 
borders, class imbalance, and lack of class consistency and border accuracy. To achieve better classification 
results, hybrid architectures like Hybrid Bayesian-Machine Learning, CNN+U-Net, CNN+ViT, CNN+LSTM, 
and U-Net+ResNet are desired1,20,21,23. Gradually, DL models have paved the way to build strong, robust frame-
works to work on multispectral and hyperspectral datasets. For instance, a study by Alkhatib 24 has presented 
a hybrid framework called “ConvVitMamba” for efficient hyperspectral image classification, mainly to dem -
onstrate land cover as urban and non-urban areas using datasets like Houston and UA V-borne QUH datasets 24. 
After conducting an extensive literature review, it was found that merging two models for crop stress and drought 
estimation combines the strengths of different architectures, offering better image processing and crop stress 
classification. They offer higher accuracy, better generalization, and improved class balance, making them ideal 
for tasks involving heterogeneous landscapes, small object detection, or imbalanced data. The aforementioned 
methods are being combined with ML and DL to increase accuracy and scalability because of the growing avail-
ability of high-resolution images from satellites such as Sentinel-22,25.
Conventional methods are time-consuming and have a small geographic coverage for identifying crop stress 
and drought stress in crops, which occur due to extreme heat, drought, nutrient shortages, or salinity 5. These 
methods have several intrinsic drawbacks that limit their efficacy, especially at the regional or national level. 
Also, conventional methods mostly rely on the observer’s experience and interpretation and are frequently sub -
jective and include visual field inspections, manual sampling, and physiological tests. They may, therefore, result 
in evaluations that are erroneous or inconsistent. These techniques are both time-consuming and labor-intensive, 
which makes them inappropriate for large-scale agricultural landscapes or frequent monitoring. Hence, to solve 
these issues, a DL-based framework for multi-temporal crop and drought stress monitoring in precision agricul -
ture has been proposed for intelligent farming as a solution to modern farming challenges, emphasizing the need 
for sustainable practices and innovative approaches to modern agriculture.

Study area and satellite images used
Study area
Haldharmau village, located in the Colonelganj Tehsil of the Gonda District, Uttar Pradesh, India, has been taken 
as the study region, which is situated at 27°08′43′′ N latitude and 81°47′41′′ E longitude. It is roughly 20 km from 
the Gonda district headquarters and 31 km from the Ghaghra River, which is the main fluvial system affecting 
the hydrology of the area. The region is located at an elevation of 180.7 m above mean sea level and consists of 
190.81 hectares of primarily agricultural land. The area has a subtropical climate, with an annual mean precipita-
tion of roughly 1552 mm, mostly during the monsoon months, and significant seasonal temperature fluctuations 
ranging from 1.0 °C in the winter to 49 °C in the height of summer. The tarhar (lowland) physiography of the 
research area is characterized by rich alluvial soils with clay loam, loam, and sandy loam textures that support a 
variety of agricultural systems. With a reported cropping intensity of 157% and almost 60% of the cultivable area 
under irrigation, agricultural techniques are intensive. Paddy, wheat, mustard, maize, and sugarcane are the main 
crops, and their seasonal rotations are influenced by irrigation availability as well as rainfall. The Ghaghara River 
and its tributaries impact soil moisture dynamics, sediment transport, and water resource distribution. Satellite 
images reveal diverse land-use mosaics, influencing crop stress and land-use change. Haldharmau village serves 
as a microcosm for assessing crop health and hydrological interactions, as shown in the India and Uttar Pradesh 
maps in Fig. 1.
In Fig. 1, the study area is shown in a false-color composite image (generated from March 2024 images of 
Sentinel-2 by assigning the red color to Band 8 (Near-Infrared), green color to Band 4 (Red), and blue color to 
Band 3 (Green)) to show the agricultural landscape with a meandering river system. The bright areas represent 
riverbanks and water channels, while the mixed tones represent land use, agricultural fields, settlements, and bare 
soil. This image is used for land cover mapping, river morphology analysis, and crop stress monitoring.
Satellite images used
Sentinel-2 satellite images have been used in this study to monitor the agricultural areas, specifically the culti -
vation and harvesting of wheat crops. The main focus is to extract information on the wheat crop’s cultivation, 
harvesting, and post-harvesting cycle. Images are collected from the Copernicus Portal from January to June 
2023, 2024, and 2025, covering the wheat crop’s cultivation, harvesting, and post-harvesting cycle, which typi -
cally occurs in November–December and is harvested in April. To develop a machine learning-based framework

Theoretical background
Pre-processing of Sentinel-2 images
Sentinel-2 multispectral Level-2A (L2A) satellite images were downloaded from the Sentinel Scientific Data 
Hub from January to June 2024. These images were pre-processed by applying atmospheric correction, geo -
metric correction, radiometric calibration, resampling, and reprojection of Geometric Latitude and Longitude 
through the SNAP tool. Atmospheric correction converts Top-of-Atmosphere (TOA) reflectance into Bottom-
of-Atmosphere (BOA) reflectance, representing the true reflectance of Earth’s surface 26. Radiometric calibra -
tion transforms raw values into physically relevant values, ensuring that brightness values accurately reflect the 
amount of light reflected or emitted from the ground. Geometric correction corrects spatial distortions due to 
Earth’s curvature, sensors’ angle, satellite motion, and terrain variations26. Resampling integrates spectral bands 
with different spatial resolutions into a uniform resolution (i.e., 10 m for each band), enabling more accurate and 
consistent analysis. Band 2 (Blue) is commonly used as the reference band for resampling to standardize the same 
spatial resolution across all bands used for further analysis. Finally, reprojection converts the UTM projection to 
the WGS 84 (Latitude/Longitude) projection. The pre-processed B4 (Red), B8 (Near-Infrared), and B11 (Short -
wave Infrared) bands of Sentinel-2 images are used to calculate vegetation indices, such as NDVI, NDWI, SA VI, 
GNDVI, and PSSR images, but in this study, NDVI, NDWI, and PSSR images have been utilized as input images 
for land cover classification and further used to retrieve the crop and drought stress maps.
The motive behind selecting these indices over SA VI and GNDVI is to eliminate redundancy of indices, as 
NDVI, NDWI, and PSSR have served the purpose for canopy structure, water content, and pigment sensitivity, 
respectively. Also, these three indices are more suitable for abiotic stress detection as discussed in 5. The details 
of NDVI, NDWI, and PSSR are given as follows:

hybrid CNN+ViT model is used on the layer-stacked NDVI, NDWI, and PSSR index images. The architecture 
of the DL-based CNN+ViT model to obtain the urban, water, bare soil, and cropland classes is shown in Fig. 3.

The integration of ViT with CNN in a hybrid architecture offers advantages in understanding complex spatial 
patterns and inter-class boundaries in multispectral Sentinel-2 images. ViT’s model uses convolutional projection 
and Multi-Head Self-Attention for spatial correlations across the entire image patch, improving discrimination 
power and robustness to class overlap. CNN has three convolutional layers with 32, 64, and 128 filters, with 
ReLU activation and max-pooling operations to capture local spatial features. In the next step, the four-class 
classification output is passed to the Softmax layer and trained using the Adam optimizer. The CNN+ViT clas -
sification model implementation details are discussed as follows:
 ● Patch & Channel Dimensions: The model processes multi-spectral input patches of size 64 × 64 pixels across 10 
spectral channels derived from Sentinel-2 surface reflectance images.
 ● Hyperparameters & Optimization: Model training is conducted using the Adam optimizer with an initial learn-
ing rate of 0.0001, a batch size of 32, and trained for 50 epochs.
 ● Loss Function: Cross-Entropy loss was employed to optimize multi-class land-cover classification.
 ● Transformer Configuration: The Vision Transformer encoder utilizes 4 Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) 
heads and 2 Transformer encoder blocks to capture long-range contextual spatial relationships.
 ● Augmentation & Early Stopping:  Data augmentation techniques (horizontal/vertical flips and random rota -
tions) are applied to enhance generalizability, combined with an early-stopping procedure based on validation 
loss monitoring with a patience of 10 epochs to prevent overfitting.
 ● Software & Hardware Environment: Experiments have been implemented in Python using the PyTorch deep 
learning framework, executed on NVIDIA GPU acceleration environments along with ESA SNAP (v10.0) and 
ENVI Classic for pre-processing and GIS mapping.

Role of crop & drought stress in precision agriculture
Precision agriculture is a crucial approach for monitoring and mitigating crop and drought stress in real time, as 
climate change increases the frequency and intensity of drought events28,29. PA integrates satellite images, machine 
learning, and IoT-based monitoring systems to enable farmers to make data-driven decisions that minimize yield 
losses and optimize resource use30,31. Various satellite images are used for yield estimation, detecting immature 
fruits, and assessing chilling, heat, and insect injuries 32. Applications include post-harvest quality control, grad-
ing, sorting, insect detection, and food safety3. In precision agriculture, successful agricultural operations depend

Development of a DL-based framework for crop & drought stress monitoring
To detect crop and drought stress in agricultural areas, a DL-based framework is required because traditional 
approaches often struggle to handle the complexity and large volume of satellite images. Sentinel-2 images 
provide high-resolution, multispectral information that can highlight minute changes in the health of the vegeta-
tion34. These multidimensional datasets can be effectively analysed by DL algorithms, which can also automati-
cally identify patterns pertaining to crop growth and stress and produce precise, timely, and scalable detection 
results. Precision farming and sustainable water resource management are made possible by this method’s data-
driven decision-making. Therefore, in this paper, a DL-based framework has been proposed for crop and drought 
stress detection to provide accurate long-term monitoring of vegetation health and water stress in agricultural 
areas. The complete workflow diagram of an adaptive deep learning framework for multi-temporal crop and 
drought stress monitoring in precision agriculture is shown in Fig. 6.
The proposed framework has been implemented by utilizing the multispectral Sentinel-2 satellite images and 
a sophisticated DL-based CNN+ViT hybrid model to monitor crop stress and drought stress. The step-by-step 
development process of the proposed framework is described in the given pseudo-code shown in Fig. 7.
The downloaded Sentinel-2 images from January to June of 2023, 2024, and 2025 have been pre-processed 
by applying radiometric calibration, atmospheric correction, and spatial resampling to a standard resolution of 
each band to 10 m. To develop the DL-based proposed framework, the March 2024 image has been taken as 
the reference image, as during this time, wheat crops are usually healthy and show minimal crop and drought 
stresses. In the next step, an efficient and accurate classified image has been obtained by applying the CNN+ViT 
classification model on a layered-stacked image of NDVI, NDWI, and PSSR indices, and then, by utilizing the 
classified image, a vegetation crop mask has been prepared by discarding the urban, water, and bare soil class 
pixels. A masked image is basically a binary image in which the cropland class is represented by 1, and other 
classes are represented by 0, because crop stress and drought stress are mapped in the cropland class only. After 
that, a SoftMax function is applied for pixel mapping, where each cropland-masked pixel is associated with its 
corresponding NDVI value. This process overlays the NDVI values onto the masked pixels to determine whether 
a given pixel represents cropland or non-cropland to generate the crop stress map. Similarly, the NDWI values 
are integrated with their respective masked pixels to classify them as cropland or non-cropland, enabling the 
development of a spatially explicit drought stress map. In the final step, crop stress monitoring is carried out by 
applying a threshold value of NDVI and NDWI given in 5,14 to the mapped NDVI and NDWI values to produce 
the crop and drought stress maps.
Pixels with NDVI values between 0.2 and 0.5 are classified as stressed crops and assigned a value of 1, while 
pixels with NDVI values greater than 0.5 are identified as healthy crops and assigned a value of 2. All remain -
ing pixels, corresponding to urban, water, or bare soil classes, are treated as background pixels and assigned a 
value of 0. Similarly, a drought stress map has been generated by applying the threshold values to the mapped 
NDWI values, where threshold values are less than 0.2, then the pixel is marked as a drought-stressed crop and 
represented by 1, while if the threshold value is greater than 0.2, then the pixel is marked as a healthy crop, and 
the remaining pixels (masked pixels of urban, water, and bare soil classes) are marked as background pixels and 
represented by 0. Subsequently, the final crop stress and drought stress maps are obtained, demonstrating the 
spatial distribution of crop health and drought stress-affected regions across the study area.
Similarly, the proposed framework is tested and validated in 2023 and 2025 images to make it an adaptive 
framework for crop and drought stress monitoring, and it is found that the proposed framework is capable of 
producing the individual crop stress and drought maps for any given Sentinel-2 image. The retrieved crop stress 
is indicated by a considerable decrease in crop area over time, whereas drought conditions are indicated by sus -
tained drops in water availability or lack of soil moisture. Crop stress and drought stress analysis is limited to 
cropland pixels using a binary spatial mask, ensuring agronomically meaningful quantification. Non-crop pixels

Performance assessment of crop and drought stress maps
The crop and drought stress values shown in Tables 2, 3, and 4 are further utilized for prediction accuracy analysis

Mean Stress, Standard Deviation, and Coefficient of Variation have been computed to evaluate the overall per-

of crop stress during each given year, whereas Standard Deviation and Coefficient of Variation present temporal 
stability and seasonal adaptability. According to Table 6, the year 2023 has shown high seasonal fluctuations; 
on the other hand, 2024 shows moderate to high seasonal fluctuations, and 2025 conveys more stable temporal 
behavior. In line with meteorological reports for North India that indicate below-normal pre-monsoon rainfall for 
2025, this early and extended stress phase in 2025 predicts a more chronic moisture deficit caused by decreased 
winter rainfall, greater temperature anomalies, and possible groundwater depletion. According to the combined 
NDVI–NDWI measures for all three years, 2025 had the most severe and long-lasting drought stress, 2023 had 
a normal seasonal moisture-exhaustion curve, and 2024 momentarily profited from pre-monsoon moisture avail-
ability in May. These findings support early-warning systems and adaptive irrigation management strategies in 
climate-vulnerable agricultural regions by validating the suggested smart-agriculture monitoring framework’s 
capacity to identify both vegetation-response stress and soil-moisture-driven drought signals.
Limitations and future work
The proposed DL-based framework can be further developed to achieve optimization by adding lightweight 
attention mechanisms and transformers variants, which will enhance its functionality and efficiency and may 
be deployed on resource-constrained agricultural monitoring systems. The proposed framework is tested and 
validated using Sentinel-2 images collected from a single geographic area and within a defined time frame (i.e., 
January to June), so its applicability to other regions, climate regimes, crop types, or growing seasons remains 
to be confirmed. Future studies may combine this with high-resolution drone images to enhance its features for 
the assessment of biotic stress, such as weed detection and other crop diseases caused by rodents. The proposed 
DL-based framework is effective for the detection of crop and drought stress. To strengthen future work, the 
framework can be extended to strengthen the functionality of the proposed model by defining the type and sever-
ity of abiotic stress.

Conclusion
The main aim of this study is the assessment of abiotic stress conditions around Haldharmau village by applying 
the proposed adaptive DL-based framework on Sentinel-2 images acquired from January to June 2023, 2024, and 
2025. Based on deep analysis, crop stress and drought conditions are influenced by methods of cultivation and 
the presence of humidity in the soil. The multi-temporal stress maps, which displayed senescence, decline, and 
widespread stress in the pre-monsoon months after peak vegetative health in February, shed light on seasonal 
crop dynamics, and after conducting a comprehensive analysis, it is observed that drought stress maps from 2023 
and 2025 show significant differences in the onset and severity of water-related crop stress. In 2023, healthy crops 
dominated the early season, but drought stress emerged gradually by March. By April, stress zones expanded, 
and by May–June, drought stress became widespread. In contrast, in 2025, stress escalated rapidly, with nearly 
four-fifths of cropland under drought stress. Hence, the proposed framework tends to be robust and scalable with 
respect to crop stress assessment and decision-making in precision agriculture, as monitoring drought stress pat-
terns is crucial for scientific understanding and food system stability. Patterns of drought stress in 2023, 2024, and 
2025 are analyzed during the study, which shows both inter-annual and continuous fluctuations. Early in 2023, 
robust vegetation predominated, but from March to June, drought stress worsened. Crop health was good in 2024, 
but in April, stress increased significantly, impacting about 99% of cropland. After February, tension expanded 
quickly in 2025 and continued into April, May, and June, leaving only sporadic areas of health. The proposed 
framework utilizes CNN+ViT classification and NDVI/NDWI-based stress mapping, capturing seasonal vegeta-
tion dynamics, revealing variations in crop health and stress levels. In 2023, vegetation declined due to increasing 
drought stress from March to June. The 2024 season saw a reduction in stress in May due to moisture influx. In 
contrast, 2025 faced early and prolonged stress conditions. The findings highlight the effectiveness of NDVI and 
NDWI in detecting stressed crops and suggest that the framework can track both short-term stress transitions 
and longer-term vulnerability trends, marking its potential for operational monitoring. The multi-year evaluation 
enhances the model’s resilience and suitability for monitoring short-term stress dynamics and long-term agricul-
tural vulnerability. The study advocates for the application of real-time remote sensing and hybrid deep learning 
to improve climate-resilient agriculture and plans to expand the framework’s applicability at the State or National 
level of agriculture monitoring in the future.
