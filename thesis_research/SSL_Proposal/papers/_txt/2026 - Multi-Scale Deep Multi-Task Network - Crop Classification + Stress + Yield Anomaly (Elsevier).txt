ABSTRACT
Agricultural monitoring in Sub-Saharan Africa faces three interlinked and operationally critical chal-
lenges: crop type identification, stress detection, and yield anomaly estimation. These are typically 
addressed in isolation, despite their physical interdependence. In semi-arid regions such as Kano State, 
Nigeria, smallholder rainfed agriculture is especially vulnerable to climate-driven water stress, making 
integrated monitoring frameworks essential for food security decision-making. Conventional single- 
task, single-sensor approaches fail to capture the combined effects of phenological diversity, soil 
moisture dynamics, and precipitation anomalies that govern productivity in these environments. This 
paper proposes a Multi-Scale Deep Multi-Task Network (MS-DMTN) that performs simultaneous crop 
classification, stress identification, and yield anomaly estimation through a novel three-stream archi-
tecture. The network integrates Sentinel-2 optical data (48 Vegetation Indices), Sentinel-1 SAR data (12 
radar features), and hydrological variables (36 climate–soil moisture features) from ERA5-Land and 
CHIRPS over Kano State. A Gated Attention Fusion Mechanism provides adaptive weighting of all input 
streams for better multi-task predictions. Stress labels were independently generated from soil moisture 
and precipitation anomalies, avoiding circular validation. Low agreement between spectral indicators 
and climate indicators (25.7%) confirms labels’ independence. Using 4734 samples across six crops and 
four stress levels, MS-DMTN outperformed baseline models and achieved 95.99% classification accu-
racy, 87.33% stress detection accuracy, and a yield R² of 0.92. Outputs demonstrated ecological 
consistency, strong yield correlations (r ≈ 0.83), and 90.7% land cover agreement. Results showed that 
hydrological data significantly improved stress detection and yield prediction, while the attention 
mechanism improved multi-source integration for scalable agriculture monitoring.

Global food security is increasingly threatened by climate change, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where rainfed smallholder 
agriculture dominates the production system (Abebe, 2021; Rembold et al., 2019). Nigeria, Africa’s most populous country with over 
200 million people, relies significantly on agricultural output from its northern savanna regions, where persistent droughts and pest

northwestern Nigeria, is an important agricultural center that sustains millions of livelihoods by cultivating essential crops such as 
sorghum, millet, maize, rice, cowpea, and groundnut (Balana et al., 2025; Chiaka et al., 2022; Ayanlade et al., 2022). As the food- 
basket of northern Nigeria, Kano is a priority target for early-warning systems that support food-security policy and humanitarian 
response. This semi-arid region shows significant seasonal variability, with annual rainfall between 800 and 1000 mm primarily 
occurring from May to October (Adelodun et al., 2021). As a result, crops are particularly vulnerable to water stress during essential 
growth periods (Adisa et al., 2018; Umar et al., 2022). Conventional methods for monitoring crops from the ground fall short for 
prompt assessments over large areas, primarily due to logistical challenges, resource limitations, and the varied nature of agricultural 
landscapes (Kpienbaareh et al., 2021; Sishodia et al., 2020). There is therefore a pressing need for scalable, cost-effective remote- 
sensing models that can simultaneously identify crop types, detect stress conditions, and estimate yield anomalies to inform early- 
warning systems and agricultural policy.
Remote sensing technologies have transformed agricultural monitoring by offering broad, consistent observations across various 
electromagnetic wavelengths (Kamilaris & Prenafeta-Boldú, 2018; Ma et al., 2019). Multispectral optical sensors, particularly those 
deployed on the European Space Agency's Sentinel−2 satellites, furnish high-resolution imagery (10–20 m) across the visible, red- 
edge, near-infrared, and shortwave infrared spectra. This capability facilitates comprehensive vegetation evaluation through the 
application of indices like the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI), and Normalized 
Difference Water Index (NDWI) (Clevers & Gitelson, 2013; Xue & Su, 2017). These spectral indices demonstrate robust correlations 
with critical crop biophysical attributes, encompassing leaf area index, chlorophyll concentration, and biomass accumulation 
(Gitelson et al., 2014). Optical remote sensing is affected by cloud cover and atmospheric interference (Whitecraft et al., 2015). 
Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) systems, exemplified by the C-band sensors on Sentinel−1, mitigate these constraints by actively 
transmitting microwave radiation. This technology allows for cloud penetration and functions without reliance on sunlight (Mascolo 
et al., 2016). SAR backscatter intensity in VV and VH polarizations reflects canopy structure, soil moisture, and vegetation water 
content, offering additional insights alongside optical observations (Mandal et al., 2020a,2020b). Integrating multi-sensor data from 
optical and radar systems offers a strong method for ongoing crop monitoring across various atmospheric conditions (Steele-Dunne 
et al., 2017).
Recent developments in deep learning have revolutionized the analysis of remote sensing images, allowing for automatic feature 
extraction from high-dimensional, multi-temporal satellite data, reducing reliance on manually created spectral indices (Joshi et al., 
2023; Yuan et al., 2020). Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and their derivatives have demonstrated considerable efficacy in 
crop classification, owing to their capacity to learn hierarchical spatial-spectral representations that effectively distinguish nuanced 
phenological variations among crop varieties (Kussul et al., 2017; Zhong et al., 2019). For multi-temporal time-series analysis, 
transformer-based architectures such as the Pixel-Set Encoder and Temporal Attention Encoder (PSE-TAE) have further improved 
crop-type mapping accuracy (Garnot et al., 2020; Rußwurm & Körner, 2020).
Most existing studies, however, address agricultural monitoring as a series of discrete tasks, encompassing crop type mapping, 
stress detection, and yield prediction, each requiring separate model training and inference procedures (Chlingaryan et al., 2018; van 
Klompenburg et al., 2020). This single-task paradigm, however, fails to account for the interdependencies inherent in agricultural 
variables: crop type influences stress susceptibility, the timing and intensity of stress directly impact yield outcomes, and yield 
anomalies often correspond to specific crop-stress interactions (Lobell et al., 2015). Multi-task learning (MTL) frameworks overcome 
this limitation by optimizing several related objectives together within a single architecture. This approach facilitates knowledge 
transfer across tasks by utilizing shared representations (Ruder, 2017; Zhang & Yang, 2021). In the fields of computer vision and 
natural language processing, multi-task learning (MTL) has shown reliable enhancements in generalization performance and para-
meter efficiency when compared to single-task models (Crawshaw, 2020; Zhang & Yang, 2021). Despite the evident advantages, the 
application of multi-task deep learning within the domain of multi-sensor agricultural remote sensing remains relatively under-
explored. Specifically, three gaps persist: (i) existing MTL studies rarely fuse optical, SAR, and hydro-climatic data within a single 
architecture; (ii) stress labels are typically derived from the same spectral indices used as inputs, producing circular validation; and 
(iii) integrated MTL benchmarks for semi-arid smallholder systems in West Africa are absent. The present study targets all three gaps.
The objective of this study is to develop and evaluate an integrated deep multi-task framework that jointly performs crop clas-
sification, stress detection, and yield anomaly estimation from fused optical, SAR, and hydro-climatic data in the semi-arid small-
holder context of Kano State, Nigeria. In particular, the study addresses three research questions: 1. Can joint learning of crop type, 
stress, and yield anomalies outperform equivalent single-task models? 2. What is the marginal contribution of each data stream 
(optical, SAR, hydro-climatic) to each task? 3. Can stress labels derived independently of spectral indices mitigate the circular- 
validation bias common in remote-sensing stress studies?
To answer these questions, we develop a Multi-Scale Deep Multi-Task Network (MS-DMTN) with a three-stream encoder for 
optical, SAR, and hydro-climatic inputs; a gated-attention fusion module for adaptive multi-source integration; and task-specific 
decoders for classification, stress, and yield built on a shared backbone trained with a weighted multi-task loss. The inputs comprise 
Sentinel−2 indices, Sentinel−1 SAR backscatter, and hydro-climatic variables from ERA5-Land and CHIRPS over Kano State. 
Crucially, stress labels are derived independently from ERA5-Land soil moisture and CHIRPS precipitation anomalies not from the 
spectral vegetation indices used as model inputs reducing the circular-validation bias that affects many remote-sensing crop-stress 
studies.
The main contributions of this paper are: (1) a three-stream multi-task network with a gated-attention fusion module that 
adaptively weights optical, SAR, and hydro-climatic streams for joint crop classification, stress detection, and yield-anomaly esti-
mation. (2) stress labels derived independently from ERA5-Land soil moisture and CHIRPS precipitation anomalies, reducing the

2. Methodology
2.1. Study area
Kano State is located between latitudes 10°30'N to 13°0′N and longitudes 7°40'E to 9°50'E in northwestern Nigeria, which spans 
around 43,285 km² (Fig. 1). The state is primarily characterized by agricultural land within the Sudan savanna agro-ecological zone. 
The area has a semi-arid tropical climate, marked by clear wet and dry seasons. The growing season lasts from May to October, during 
which monthly rainfall varies between 100 and 250 mm. The average annual temperatures range from 24 to 28°C, with maximum 
temperatures surpassing 40°C in the pre-monsoon months of March to May, which exposes crops to heat stress. Agricultural activity 
within Kano State is concentrated in the Sudan savanna zone, where rainfed production systems dominate both lowland fadama and 
upland areas. Crop assemblages vary spatially with soil type and proximity to seasonal rivers. Fadama (low-lying floodplains) support 
rice cultivation, while upland sandy loam soils favour sorghum, millet, cowpea, and groundnut. These landscape-level variations 
motivate a spatially distributed remote sensing approach capable of discriminating fine-scale crop-type differences. Smallholder 
farms, typically ranging from 0.5 to 2 ha, depend on conventional rainfed agricultural practices. This reliance exposes the region to 
heightened susceptibility to climatic variations and seasonal droughts during critical periods of crop development. Kano State was 
chosen for this study due to its agricultural importance, the persistent stress patterns observed in agricultural datasets, and the 
accessibility of clear satellite imagery during essential growth phases. The geographical position and administrative boundaries of the 
study area are illustrated in Fig. 1, which presents (a) the detailed local government structure of Kano State with major towns and the 
state capital, (b) the location of Nigeria within the African continent, and (c) Kano State's position within Nigeria.
2.2. Remote sensing data acquisition and preprocessing
2.2.1. Sentinel−1 SAR data
Sentinel−1 Ground Range Detected (GRD) products in Interferometric Wide (IW) swath mode offered additional radar ob-
servations unaffected by cloud interference, obtained from GEE for the same time frame. Data filtering focused on retaining only VV 
and VH dual-polarization acquisitions. This is because the cross-polarized VH backscatter shows greater sensitivity to vegetation 
structure and moisture than the co-polarized VV alone (Bauer-Marschallinger et al., 2021). SAR preprocessing involved removing 
thermal noise, calibrating radiometrically to the sigma-nought backscatter coefficient, and correcting terrain using the SRTM 30 m 
digital elevation model (Small, 2011) to address topographic distortions in the radar geometry. The backscatter intensities measured

2.2.2. Sentinel−2 multispectral imagery
Sentinel−2 Level−2A data surface reflectance imagery was obtained via the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform (Gorelick et al., 
2017; Drusch et al., 2012) for the period from 2020 to 2024. This imagery offers bottom-of-atmosphere reflectance that has been 
corrected for atmospheric effects using the Sen2Cor algorithm (Main-Knorn et al., 2017). The Sentinel−2 constellation revisits the 
equator every 5 days, allowing for timely observations that are essential for monitoring quick changes in vegetation throughout the 
growing season. Images are selected using strict quality filters, keeping only those with cloud coverage below 20%. Cloud masking 
employed the QA60 band, where bits 10 and 11 signify opaque clouds and cirrus, respectively. Pixels marked by either bit are 
excluded from further analysis. We retained ten spectral bands covering wavelengths from 443 to 2190 nm: Blue (B2, 10 m), Green 
(B3, 10 m), Red (B4, 10 m), Red-Edge 1–3 (B5-B7, 20 m), Near-Infrared (B8, 10 m), Narrow NIR (B8A, 20 m), and Shortwave Infrared 
1–2 (B11-B12, 20 m). Surface reflectance values were adjusted to a range of 0–1 by dividing by 10,000.
Eight vegetation and spectral stress indices were computed per pixel to enhance biophysical information content; their formulas 
and physical interpretations are summarized in Table 2.
All indices exploit specific spectral band combinations to amplify signals related to crop physiological status while attenuating 
confounding factors such as atmospheric scattering and soil background reflectance.
2.2.3. Hydro-climatic data integration
To fill the identified gap in hydrologic monitoring and to provide physically independent stress indicators, three hydro-climatic 
datasets were combined with the optical and SAR measurements. Note that, hydro-climatic variables are independent of spectral- 
index-based labels but still part of the feature set.
These included the following:
ERA5-Land Re-Analysis (monthly averages of three surface hydro-meteorological variables): Air Temperature at 2 m (tempera-
ture_2m in Kelvin), Total Precipitation (total_precipitation in mm), Volumetric Soil Moisture (in m3 / m3) in the surface layer 
(volumetric_soil_water_layer_1) of the 2020–2024 time frame. Spatial Resolution is approximately 9 kilometers. The ERA5-Land Re- 
Analysis is a physically consistent, global re-analysis product utilizing both model physics and observational constraints (Muñoz- 
Sabater et al., 2021). This makes it a good proxy for soil moisture and precipitation as indicators of agricultural water availability. 
Monthly median values were calculated over the six-month growing seasons (May–October) for each variable to create 18 ERA5-Land 
features per sample (3 variables × 6 months).

(SM_BL_M5–M10) were computed and used to derive current-year anomalies. A total of twelve additional features were added.
2.2.4. Multi-temporal feature extraction
A multi-temporal approach to extracting features is essential to capturing the essential phenological dynamics related to crop type 
classification and stress detection. Therefore, monthly median composites are created for each of the months of May through October 
to represent the monthly median composite for each growing season month (the period of time from planting until maturity).
Compositing monthly data serves two purposes. It first helps remove residual cloud contamination in optical data. Second, it helps 
reduce SAR speckle noise through temporal averaging while providing enough temporal resolution to track growth stage progression. 
To create a feature vector for each sample point, the following are stacked together: 
1. Eight different vegetation indices from Sentinel−2 across the six months (yielding 48 optical features);
2. Two polarization channels from Sentinel−1 SAR (VV, VH) across the same six months (yielding 12 SAR features);
3. Thirty-six hydro-climatic features previously described (resulting in a comprehensive 96 dimensional multi-temporal, multi- 
source feature space that is organized into three separate modality streams).
The three modality streams preserve the physical meaning and statistical properties of each data source. However, they also 
enable modality specific encoding before fusion. To extract the features from the data, the sampleRegions function from Google Earth 
Engine was used with a 100 m spatial scale. This scale was selected as a practical compromise across the three input datasets. It 
exceeds the Sentinel−1 (10 m) and Sentinel−2 (10–20 m) pixel sizes, allowing minor co-registration offsets between sensors to be 
absorbed. At the same time, it remains well below the ERA5-Land (∼9 km) and CHIRPS (∼5 km) grid-cell sizes, where point 
sampling and 100 m sampling are equivalent in practice. The 100 m radius is also smaller than the smallest typical smallholder field 
in Kano State (∼0.5 ha ≈ 70 m side), reducing mixed-pixel contamination at field boundaries. The sampleRegions function extracts 
the mean value of each pixel within a sample region to account for minor geolocation errors and sub-pixel heterogeneity.
2.3. Sample data generation and labeling
Due to the lack of large-scale ground truth surveys over the 43,285 km² of Kano state, 5000 stratified random sample points were 
generated throughout the study area using the stratifiedSample function in Google Earth Engine with a fixed random seed (42) to 
ensure reproducibility. After the sample points were evaluated for quality, those with missing data or with only bare soil signatures 
(NDVImax < 0.15) were removed. As a result, 4734 valid samples of agriculture were available for further analysis. Labels for the 
three tasks were generated from different datasets to avoid circular validation.
Samples were assigned to one of the six crop classes (sorghum, millet, maize, rice, cowpea, groundnut) using a multi-source 
decision tree that jointly incorporated growing-season vegetation indices, SAR backscatter statistics, and mean soil moisture, fol-
lowing the multi-indicator labeling approach described in Kpienbaareh et al. (2021) and Orynbaikyzy et al. (2020). Incorporating 
multiple data streams into the labeling scheme ensures that no single sensor alone contains all the discriminative information needed 
to reproduce the labels, preserving the validity of the multi-source fusion evaluation.
Importantly, the stress labels were derived entirely from hydro-climatic variables, soil moisture anomalies from ERA5-Land and 
CHIRPS precipitation departures, and they were independent of the spectral vegetation indices that served as input to the optical 
models. Monthly soil moisture anomalies were calculated as departures from study-period baseline averages (SM_anom_Mm = 
SM_current_Mm − SM_LT_Mm), and precipitation anomalies were calculated as relative departures ((CHIRPS − CHIRPS_BL) / 
CHIRPS_BL). Four stress classes were defined based on percentile-based thresholds: Severe Drought (SM anomaly < 15th percentile 
AND precipitation anomaly < 20th percentile), Moderate Drought (SM anomaly < 35th percentile OR precipitation anomaly < 20th 
percentile), Heat Stress (2 m temperature exceeds 75th percentile in the absence of moisture deficit), and No Stress (all anomalies are 
within normal ranges). Independence between stress labels and spectral inputs was confirmed by agreement analysis. Agreement 
between climate-based stress labels and spectral-index-based labels (based on MSI quartiles) was only 25.7%, confirming that the 
model cannot produce high stress accuracy by merely learning spectral index thresholds.
Yield targets were formulated as a blended anomaly index: yield = 0.6 × climate_yield + 0.4 × spectral_yield, where climate_yield in-
tegrates growing-season soil moisture and precipitation anomalies, and spectral_yield reflects peak NDVI as a proxy for photosynthetic 
capacity and end-of-season biomass (Jin et al., 2017; Lobell et al., 2015). The 60/40 climate-to-spectral weighting reflects the dominant role 
of water availability in determining productivity in semi-arid rainfed systems (Cheng et al., 2022; Tripathi et al., 2022). The blended index 
was normalized to the [−1, + 1] range to represent relative yield anomalies, consistent with practice in regional early-warning applications 
(Rembold et al., 2019).

2.4. Data partitioning & preprocessing
The 4734 valid samples were partitioned into training (n = 2840, 60%), validation (n = 947, 20%), and testing (n = 947, 20%) 
sets using stratified random sampling based on crop type labels, ensuring that all six crop classes were represented proportionally 
across every split. We standardized the features of the dataset by applying Z-score normalization (zero mean/unit variance) to each of 
the 96 features separately. The scaling factors for this were calculated using the training data only and then applied uniformly to the 
validation and test data. As we had integer class labels for the two classification tasks, we utilized sparse categorical cross entropy 
loss. For the yield anomaly regression task, the target values for yield anomalies retained their normalized continuous value for Mean 
Squared Error calculation. Each of the three feature subsets was treated as a separate input stream to the network; i.e., optical 
features (48 dimensional), SAR features (12 dimensional), and hydrological features (36 dimensional). Each input stream had its own 
independent encoder pathway leading to the gated fusion component.

2.7. Baseline models for comparison
In order to thoroughly assess the benefits of MS-DMTN's multi-stream, multi-task learning, we have trained several categories of 
single-task baseline models, all of which use only the optical features (input dimension 48). There are many scientific reasons why we 
have chosen to limit our baselines to optical-only inputs. First, the baseline models represent the common single-stream paradigm 
found in previous studies of crop monitoring using optical remote sensing. Second, by limiting our baselines to optical-only inputs, we 
can isolate the benefits of MS-DMTN's multi-source fusion and multi-task architecture.
Single-Task DNN (ST-DNN): A shallow feedforward neural network with a single hidden layer (48 → 32 → output) trained 
individually for each task using the Adam optimizer (initial learning rate = 0.001), a maximum of 50 epochs, batch size 64, and 
early-stopping (patience = 10). The deliberately shallow architecture of this baseline is representative of many baseline architectures 
for remote sensing classification.
Random Forest (RF): An ensemble of 80 decision trees with a maximum depth of 6 trained separately for crop classification, stress 
classification, and yield regression using the 48 optical features. Random Forest is a strong non-neural baseline commonly used in 
remote sensing.
Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP): A single-hidden-layer network (48 → 32 → output) trained for 80 epochs with batch size 64, a 
minimalist neural baseline.
We compared these baselines against MS-DMTN on the same test set (n = 947) with the same evaluation metrics. Therefore, this 
comparison framework isolates three distinct advantages of the proposed approach: (1) the ability to integrate data from multiple 
sources (96 features vs. 48 optical-only), (2) modality-specific encoding with learned fusion (vs. concatenation or single-stream), and 
(3) the benefits of multi-task regularization (vs. independent task training).
2.8. Ablation study design
To measure the contributions of each data stream and the gated-fusion mechanism of MS-DMTN, we performed five ablation 
experiments using the MS-DMTN architecture, with each experiment including a modification to the original architecture:

(1) Optical-only: Only the optical encoder receives input; SAR and hydrological encoders receive no input. This isolates the con-

(2) SAR-only: Only the SAR encoder receives input. This measures if the radar backscatter itself is sufficient for distinguishing

(3) Hydro-only: Only the hydrological encoder receives input. This evaluates the predictive capability of the hydrological stream

(4) No-Gated-Fusion: All three encoders receive input, but the gated attention mechanism is replaced with a simple concatenation

(5) Full MS-DMTN: The full three-stream architecture with gated attention fusion serves as the reference configuration.
Each ablation variant was trained from scratch with the same hyperparameters (maximum number of epochs = 250, batch 
size = 32, Adam optimizer) and evaluated on the same test set.
2.9. Evaluation metrics and statistical testing
Model performance was measured using task-specific metrics on the held-out test set (n = 947). Overall accuracy, precision, 
recall, and F1 score were used for crop and stress classification, calculated using weighted averages across classes. In addition to 
overall accuracy, we also computed the precision, recall, and F1 score for each class to perform a more detailed analysis. For yield 
regression, we used the RMSE, MAE, and R². To determine the statistical significance of the performance difference between MS- 
DMTN and each of the baseline models, we used McNemar's test (Congalton & Green, 2019) for paired classification predictions (to 
determine if the two models produce different error patterns) and the Wilcoxon signed rank test (Hollander et al., 2013) for re-
gression residuals. We chose a significance level of α = 0.01 due to the multiple comparisons.
To facilitate consistent interpretation of model performance, we adopt the following qualitative threshold descriptors for each 
metric, consistent with conventions used in remote sensing crop classification literature (Congalton & Green, 2019; Foody, 2002):
For classification accuracy and F1-score: values below 0.60 are considered poor; 0.60–0.75 fair; 0.75–0.90 good; and above 0.90 
excellent. These thresholds reflect the practical requirements for operational crop monitoring systems in which misclassification has 
downstream consequences for early warning and resource allocation.
For yield regression (R²): values below 0.50 indicate poor predictive power; 0.50–0.70 moderate; 0.70–0.90 good; and above 0.90 
excellent, indicating that the model accounts for more than 90% of observed yield variability. For RMSE and MAE, lower values 
indicate better predictive accuracy; results are interpreted relative to the yield anomaly scale of [−1, + 1], with RMSE <  0.15 
considered acceptable for regional monitoring applications.
For statistical significance, a threshold of α = 0.01 (rather than the conventional 0.05) was adopted to account for multiple 
comparisons across six tests, reducing the family-wise error rate (Congalton & Green, 2019).
2.10. Feature importance analysis
To understand which features contribute to the model's predictions for each task and data stream, we trained Random Forest 
models on the optical features as surrogate models for estimating feature importance. Each decision tree in the Random Forest 
ensemble provides a Gini importance score measuring the total reduction in impurity (for classification) or variance (for regression) 
attributed to each feature across all trees. We trained separate Random Forest classifiers (80 trees, max depth = 6) for crop and stress 
tasks and a Random Forest regressor for yield prediction. We analyzed the feature importances by temporal period (from May 
through October) and feature category (vegetation indices) to identify the critical observation periods and spectral regions.
2.11. Implementation details
Data acquisition and preprocessing were performed on the Google Earth Engine cloud platform (Gorelick et al., 2017). Deep 
learning models were implemented in TensorFlow/Keras; classical baseline models used scikit-learn; and statistical hypothesis tests 
used scipy.stats. Fixed random seeds (seed = 42) were set consistently across all libraries to ensure full reproducibility.
2.12. External ground truth validation framework
In order to establish reliability, the outputs of the model were evaluated using various independently gathered datasets that 
encompass crop distribution, yield, drought occurrences, and land cover. A total of seven reference sources were utilized, enabling a 
thorough cross-examination of essential variables such as crop type, stress levels, and yield anomalies in Kano State.
In examining crop distribution, we compared satellite-derived crop proportions with three key datasets. The first is the Nigeria Bureau of 
Statistics (NBS) Annual Abstract of Statistics (2020), which provides information on harvested crop areas. The second dataset is the IFPRI

into approximately 10 km grids. Lastly, we referenced the World Bank LSMS-ISA Nigeria General Household Survey Panel Wave 4 
(2018–2019) (World Bank, 2020; DOI: 10.48529/1hgw-dq47), which contains geo-referenced, field-verified crop data collected from around 
600 plots in the North-West region. Pearson and Spearman correlations were utilized to assess the level of agreement.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Modeling training dynamics
The MS-DMTN three-stream model was trained for up to 250 epochs while using an early stopping mechanism (patience = 25) to track 
the validation loss. As shown in Fig. 3, the total multi-task loss decreased as the training progressed, while the training versus validation 
metrics demonstrated a degree of controlled overfitting. In particular, the validation accuracy for crop classification increased from ap-
proximately 61.7% (at epoch 1) to over 90% when the model reached convergence, while the validation accuracy for stress classification 
went from 61.1% to approximately 89%. Finally, the yield Mean Absolute Error (MAE) decreased from 0.283 to less than 0.10. Each task- 
specific loss trajectory indicated that the crop classification task converged quickly during the initial epochs, while the stress classification task 
required additional epochs to reach convergence due to the reliance of this task on the hydrological encoder pathway. Conversely, the yield 
regression task continued to improve throughout the duration of the training process, consistent with the hierarchical nature of each of these 
tasks and the fact that the 60/40 climate-spectral blended yield target requires the integration of information from all three data streams.

3.2. Overall model performance on test set
To assess the overall performance of the model on the test data (n = 947) we compared MS-DMTN to three baselines (ST-DNN, 
Random Forest, and Multi Layer Perceptron — MLP) on a 48 dimensional vector based on only the optical features (Table 3). In each 
metric evaluated, MS-DMTN performed better than every other baseline.
Crop classification task: MS-DMTN performed 95.99% accurate, with weighted precision of 95.97%, weighted recall of 95.99%, 
and weighted F1 score of 95.97%. ST-DNN, the next best performing baseline performed 89.65% (F1 = 89.32%) followed by RF at 
89.23% (F1 = 87.47%) and MLP at 71.07% (F1 = 69.79%). Therefore, the difference in 6.34% points when comparing MS-DMTN to 
ST-DNN and 24.92% points when comparing MS-DMTN to MLP indicates that the multi-source integration provides value. For 
context, previous studies using Sentinel−2 for crop mapping have reported accuracies in the 75–92% range (Zhao et al., 2021; Feng 
et al., 2019), and Wang et al. (2021)achieved 83.4% using attention-based CNNs with Sentinel−2 time series alone. However, direct 
comparison with these studies is limited by differences in study area, crop types, number of classes, and critically, ground truth 
methodology, those studies relied on field-verified labels, whereas the present study uses multi-source decision-tree-derived labels. 
The higher accuracy achieved by MS-DMTN partly reflects the multi-source crop labeling strategy: because crop assignment in-
corporates SAR backscatter (mean VH, standard deviation of VV) and soil moisture alongside vegetation indices, the classification 
task inherently requires multi-stream integration and cannot be fully replicated by any optical-only baseline. This labeling approach 
strengthens the evaluation of multi-source fusion benefits but should not be interpreted as a direct methodological improvement over 
field-validated crop mapping studies.
Stress classification Task: MS-DMTN achieved an accuracy of 87.33% with a weighted F1 score of 88.18%, significantly out-
performing all of the baselines: RF (78.35%), MLP (66.95%), and ST-DNN (65.26%). The reason for such a significant improvement in 
accuracy is the inclusion of the hydrologic encoder, which provided ERA5 soil moisture anomalies and CHIRPS precipitation de-
partures. These are the exact variables used to derive independent stress labels. Therefore, the use of these variables as inputs into the 
stress detection model ensures that the accuracy of stress detection is truly related to the relationship between soil moisture and stress 
rather than being related to circular spectral correlation. Furthermore, the 87.33% accuracy is greater than that found in the lit-
erature: Virnodkar et al. (2020)reported a range of 72–85% for crop stress detection; however, they noted that previous studies had 
difficulty detecting crop stress because of the spectral overlap that occurs among crops. Our method does not have the same issue 
because our stress indicator is climate-based rather than spectral based.
Yield Estimation task: MS-DMTN achieved RMSE of 0.11, R² of 0.92, and an MAE of 0.08, outperforming MLP (R² 0.35), ST-DNN 
(R² 0.91, the best baseline), and RF (R² 0.84). The R² improvement of + 0.07 over RF and + 0.01 over ST-DNN compares favorably 
with yield prediction literature. MS-DMTN and the best single-task baseline (ST-DNN) both achieved RMSE = 0.11; the primary 
yield-estimation gain is therefore captured by the R² improvement from 0.91 to 0.92, reflecting a modest but consistent increase in 
explained yield variance rather than a reduction in absolute error.

3.6. Comparative performance and hydrological contributions
Fig. 7 presents the comparative performance of MS-DMTN against all baseline models across the three tasks. The three-stream 
model incorporating hydro-climatic variables demonstrated consistent superiority: crop classification accuracy improved by + 6.34% 
points over the best-performing baseline (ST-DNN, 89.65%), stress detection accuracy improved by + 8.98 points over Random 
Forest (78.35%), and yield R² improved by + 0.0118 over ST-DNN (0.91).
The stress classification margin is particularly noteworthy because it reflects the direct contribution of the hydrological encoder. 
The optical-only baselines lack information about soil moisture anomalies and precipitation, the physical drivers of agricultural 
drought stress in the semi-arid regions of Kano State. By incorporating ERA5-Land soil moisture and CHIRPS precipitation data, the 
hydrological encoder enables MS-DMTN to identify stress conditions that purely spectral approaches cannot detect. This finding 
underscores the importance of integrating hydro-climatic reanalysis products into crop monitoring frameworks: soil moisture dy-
namics and precipitation patterns represent a primary signal for identifying agricultural stress and estimating yields in water-limited 
environments.
The consistent outperformance across all three tasks demonstrates that the multi-task architecture does not sacrifice performance 
on any individual objective. Rather, the three-stream design produces complementary contributions: optical features capture phe-
nological patterns for crop type identification, SAR captures structural information to support crop discrimination under cloudy 
conditions, and hydro-climatic features provide the moisture and precipitation context necessary for stress and yield assessment. 
These findings align with Lin et al. (2022)and Nguyen et al. (2023), who demonstrated that multi-task and multi-source architectures 
achieve higher performance when task-relevant data streams are explicitly incorporated.

3.8. Ablation study: data stream contributions
Ablation experiments (Table 6, Fig. 9) systematically measured the contribution of each data stream and the gated fusion me-
chanism to MS-DMTN's multi-task performance.
In the case of Optical-only (excluding SAR and Hydro encoders the crop classification accuracy remained high at 96.52%, 
marginally exceeding the full model (95.99%), confirming that optical vegetation indices carry the primary discriminative signal for 
crop type differentiation. However, stress accuracy decreased to 85.85% (−1.48 points relative to the full model) and yield R² fell to 
0.86 (−0.06), indicating that spectral indices alone cannot capture the soil moisture and precipitation dynamics that govern agri-
cultural stress and productivity.
For the SAR-only (using only radar backscatter) the crop classification accuracy dropped to 83.21%, stress to 82.26%, and yield R² 
to 0.65. While C-band SAR provides moderate crop differentiation through canopy structural differences, it lacks the spectral phe-
nological diversity needed for six-class discrimination and the environmental signal of climate-driven stresses.
Furthermore, for the Hydro-only (using ERA5-Land + CHIRPS only), this configuration produced the most scientifically sig-
nificant findings. As expected, crop classification was limited (74.76%), since climate variables alone cannot distinguish crop-specific 
growth stages. However, stress detection accuracy reached 93.03%, notably exceeding the full MS-DMTN model (87.33%), and yield 
R² reached 0.90. These results demonstrate that soil moisture anomalies and precipitation departures contain the majority of in-
formation relevant to detecting agricultural stress and estimating yields in this semi-arid environment.
The outperformance of the Hydro-only configuration over the full model for stress detection warrants explanation. Three factors 
likely contribute to this result. First, stress labels were derived directly from hydro-climatic variables (ERA5-Land soil moisture 
anomalies and CHIRPS precipitation departures); consequently, the Hydro-only encoder has direct access to the physical variables 
from which the labels were generated, providing a near-optimal input-to-label mapping for this specific task. Second, in the full 
model, the multi-task loss function assigns the highest weight to crop classification (w_crop = 2.0 versus w_stress = 1.5), which 
biases the shared representation toward spectral-phenological features that benefit crop discrimination at the expense of the hy-
drological features most relevant to stress detection — a phenomenon known as negative transfer or gradient interference in multi- 
task learning (Crawshaw, 2020). Third, the gated attention mechanism must balance stream weighting across all three tasks si-
multaneously; for stress detection alone, the optimal strategy would be to attend almost exclusively to the hydrological stream, but 
the task-shared gates cannot fully specialize. Critically, the full model's advantage lies in yield estimation (R² = 0.92), where the 
integration of all three data streams provides complementary information that no single-stream can match. This trade-off illustrates a 
well-documented property of multi-task learning: joint optimization may sacrifice peak performance on individual tasks in exchange 
for improved generalization across the full task set (Kendall et al., 2018).

model. However, yield R² dropped sharply to 0.8612 (−0.06), demonstrating that the gated attention mechanism provides its 
greatest benefit for yield estimation, where adaptive weighting of soil moisture, spectral, and SAR signals is most critical. The relative

3.9. Statistical significance and label independence
The results from formal hypothesis tests are presented in Table 7, confirming that MS-DMTN’s superior performance compared to 
each other method is statistically significant. McNemar’s test for crop classification was conducted comparing MS-DMTN to ST-DNN 
(χ² = 34.81, p = 3.64 × 10−9) and to RF (χ² = 34.81, p = 3.62 × 10−9), both significant at α = 0.01. Additionally, the McNemar’s 
test for stress classification compared MS-DMTN to ST-DNN (χ² = 130.71, p  <  10−20) and to RF (χ² = 32.82, p = 1.01 ×10−8), 
both highly significant. Finally, the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test for Yield compared MS-DMTN to ST-DNN (W = 203,421, p = 0.01) 
and to RF (W = 164,004, p = 7.07 × 10−13). Out of six comparisons, four were significant at α = 0.01, and the comparison of MS- 
DMTN to ST-DNN for yield was significant at α = 0.05 (p = 0.01) but not at α = 0.01 (W = 203,421, p = 0.01). The marginal yield 
significance relative to ST-DNN is consistent with the modest R² improvement (+0.01) between the two models, both of which 
already achieve high predictive accuracy.
Validation of the lack of dependence on climate-based stress labels in Table 8 confirmed that climate-based labels cannot be 
derived from spectral information. Binary agreement between climate-based stress indicators and spectral-based indicators was 
69.8% with a Cohen's kappa of 0.1704 (slight agreement), which indicates no relationship between the two. Also, the correlations of 
NDWI with soil-moisture (−0.021) and CHIRPS (0.009) were negligible. The overlap of drought classes was only 16.2%. Therefore, 
the 87.33% stress accuracy of MS-DMTN cannot be explained by circular validation, but rather because it uses a multi-stream 
approach to learn to identify hydro-climatically defined stress conditions.

4. Conclusion
This paper developed and tested a Multi-Scale Deep Multi-Task Network (MS-DMTN) as an integrated, three-channel network for 
full-spectrum agricultural monitoring using optical imagery from Sentinel−2, SAR imagery from Sentinel−1, and hydro-climatic 
information from ERA5-Land and CHIRPS over Kano State in Nigeria. In this case, the MS-DMTN framework integrates three separate 
but interdependent crop monitoring tasks - crop type classification, stress-level detection, and yield anomaly detection - into a 
common deep-learning architecture. Each task is addressed separately using dedicated encoders to process the input data streams; the 
resulting representations are then fused using a gated attention mechanism. Finally, each output is processed independently using the 
respective decoder(s) trained with a weighted multi-task loss function. Using the 4734 dataset containing georeferenced sample

accuracy (which is greater than 89.65% achieved by ST-DNN, p = 3.64 × 10⁻⁹); 87.33% accurate identification of stress condition

(p = 0.01) when estimating yields from its training data, with R² = 0.92 and RMSE = 0.11. Therefore, based on the results shown 
here, three-stream multi-source integration coupled with multi-task learning appears to be the most effective approach for com-
prehensive crop monitoring compared to single-stream approaches.
Systematic ablation experiments demonstrated that hydrological data play a key role in agricultural monitoring. The hydro-only 
configuration (ERA5-Land soil moisture + CHIRPS precipitation alone) obtained 93.03% accuracy for stress detection and 0.90 R² for 
yield estimation. These results indicate that soil moisture anomalies and precipitation departures are the main sources of prediction 
for detecting agricultural stress and estimating yields in semi-arid environments. Optical-only models were able to obtain high crop 
classification accuracy (96.52%), but they were degraded for stress (85.85%) and yield (R² = 0.86). These results confirm that 
spectral indices alone are unable to provide a complete representation of the physical mechanisms that govern agricultural drought. 
SAR-only models had moderate standalone performance (83.21% crop) and contributed additional structural information in the 
context of the full fusion framework. Gated attention mechanisms produced the greatest improvements for yield estimation (R² = 
0.92 vs 0.86 without gating) and thus validate the use of adaptive stream weighting for multi-sensor integration. An analysis of 
feature importance indicated that the optical vegetation indices (NDVI, EVI) from the peak growing season (August-September) 
dominated crop discrimination. A systematic ablation study demonstrates that the hydrological stream is the dominant stream for 
both stress and yield tasks.
An important methodological innovation was the development of independently derived hydro-climatically defined stress labels 
based on ERA5-Land soil moisture anomalies and CHIRPS precipitation departures, replacing the previously used spectral-defined 
stress labels. Cross-validation demonstrated the independence of the labels: the Cohen’s kappa statistic between the spectral-defined 
and climate-defined stress indicators was only 0.17 (slight agreement), the correlation between NDWI and soil moisture was neg-
ligible (−0.021), and the percentage overlap of drought classes was 16.2%. Validation by comparing to other sources of "ground 
truth" from seven other independent databases, provided a second level of verification that RS-generated crop-type labels are eco-
logically plausible: RS-derived crop-type proportions showed positive correlations with National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) census 
figures, IFPRI SPAM 2020, LSMS-ISA field survey data, and FAOSTAT national statistics (Pearson r = 0.47–0.67); however, as re-
ported in Section 3.10, these correlations did not reach conventional statistical significance given the limited number of crop classes 
compared (n = 6; p = 0.145–0.343). Compositional agreement assessed via cosine similarity (0.91–0.94) provides stronger evidence 
of directional consistency across sources. Yield Anomalies also had statistically significant correlation to both NBS Kano Yields (R = 
0.84, P = 0.04) and FAO STAT National Yields (R = 0.83, P = 0.04), for all years studied (2020–2024). Stress Prevalence identified 
through Remote Sensing techniques as being present at approximately 46.2% is consistent with the 5-year average reported by FEWS 
NET for IPC classification. There were confirmed droughts in 4 out of 5 study years according to EM-DAT. Approximately 90.7% of 
sample locations represented agricultural land in ESA World Cover 2021. The external validation used in this paper will help to 
ensure that any performance by MS-DMTN results from actual training on the characteristics of agriculture as they exist in the real 
world, as opposed to artificial characteristics generated artificially.
5. Limitations and future work
This study had the following limitations: First, the yield-anomaly target was constructed with a 40% weight on peak NDVI, which 
partially overlaps with the optical input stream; this introduces a degree of circularity that may inflate the reported R² relative to 
what would be obtained with a fully independent yield dataset (e.g. plot-level harvest records). Second, reference samples used for 
training and testing were obtained by stratified random sampling from existing crop-type and land-cover products rather than from 
in-situ field observations, so classification errors in those source layers propagate, unquantified, into MS-DMTN. Third, the test set is 
unbalanced, with only 28 samples for sorghum and 15 samples for the severe-drought class, which widens the confidence intervals 
around the class-wise accuracies and limits the strength of inference for these minority categories. Fourth, the monthly temporal 
resolution of the input composites and ERA5-Land/CHIRPS aggregates cannot capture sub-monthly phenomena such as flash 
droughts, heatwave pulses or short dry spells that are known to drive stress in semi-arid cropping systems. Fifth, FEWS NET IPC 
classifications and EM-DAT drought records used in this study operate at regional to national scales and do not provide field-level or 
pixel-level stress labels; they therefore serve as broad consistency checks for stress prevalence and temporal drought occurrence 
patterns rather than as rigorous ground-truth validation of individual pixel classifications. Finally, residual noise from Sentinel−1 
SAR speckle and from Sentinel−2 gaps under persistent cloud cover is partly mitigated but not eliminated by our preprocessing.
Building on these limitations, future work will (i) conduct GPS-referenced field-validation surveys, including in-situ measure-
ments of LAI, canopy chlorophyll and volumetric soil moisture, to provide an independent benchmark for the optical-derived yield 
target and address the circularity noted above; (ii) replace the fixed task-loss weights with an uncertainty-weighted multi-task loss to 
automate task-balancing; (iii) extend MS-DMTN into an explicitly spatio-temporal architecture (e.g. temporal Transformer or 3D- 
CNN backbone) to model dependencies between observations rather than treating the time dimension through feature engineering; 
(iv) compare the hydrological-stream soil-moisture signal against station data and SMAP retrievals.
Beyond the specific model presented here, our results carry a broader implication for agricultural remote sensing and hydrology: 
hydro-climatic reanalyses (ERA5-Land soil moisture, CHIRPS precipitation) should be treated as essential inputs not optional sup-
plements for crop monitoring in semi-arid environments. The finding that the hydrological stream alone explains the majority of

reanalysis products continue to improve in spatial resolution and accuracy, compact multi-stream architectures capable of jointly
