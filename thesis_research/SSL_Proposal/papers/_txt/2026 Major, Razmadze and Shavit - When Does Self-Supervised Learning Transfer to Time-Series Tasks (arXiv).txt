When Does Self-Supervised Learning Transfer to
Time-Series Tasks?
Noam Major Kathy Razmadze Yoli Shavit
Bar-Ilan University, Ramat-Gan, Israel
Abstract
Self-supervised learning (SSL) assumes that solving pretext tasks on unlabeled
data yields representations that transfer effectively across downstream applications
via linear probing or fine-tuning. While this paradigm has driven major progress in
vision and language, its benefits for time series remain under-investigated and often
confounded by inconsistent experimental controls. To address this gap, we bench-
mark seven representative methods from five key SSL paradigms across anomaly
detection, classification, and forecasting under parameter- and data-matched bud-
gets. We find that transfer efficacy depends heavily on the downstream task. SSL
yields substantial gains in anomaly detection and provides effective initializations
for classification under fine-tuning, but offers limited to no advantage over non-pre-
trained controls in forecasting. Furthermore, linear probing does not reliably predict
fine-tuning performance, synthetic pre-training is often competitive with real-world
corpora, and scaling encoder depth degrades forecasting accuracy. We synthesize
these empirical results into practical evaluation and development guidelines for
time-series SSL.
1 Introduction
The success of self-supervised learning (SSL) in natural language processing (Devlin et al., 2019)
and computer vision (Caron et al., 2021; Assran et al., 2023) has motivated its widespread adoption
for time-series analysis (Nie et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025; Das et al., 2024; Ansari et al., 2024).
SSL relies on the foundational premise that models can learn underlying structures and semantic
relationships from unlabeled data that transfer effectively to downstream tasks. While this premise is
well established in language and vision, its validity in time series remains less clear due to substantial
cross-domain distribution shifts and diverse temporal dynamics.
To date, empirical evidence supporting time-series SSL remains fragmented. Much of the recent
progress in time-series foundation models has been demonstrated in forecasting and, in particular,
under zero-shot evaluation (Das et al., 2024; Ansari et al., 2024). Beyond this narrow task scope,
newly proposed time-series SSL methods often introduce specialized backbones, making it difficult
to attribute observed performance gains to the SSL objective itself. Existing surveys on time-
series SSL provide informative high-level taxonomies of contrastive, generative, and reconstruction
methods (Zhang et al., 2024; Trirat et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2024), but generally do not provide
controlled, cross-task experiments that decouple the contribution of the pre-training objective from
confounding variations in model architecture, training data, and evaluation protocols. Furthermore,
latent-space alignment frameworks, such as invariance-based methods (e.g., DINO (Caron et al.,
2021)) and Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (e.g., JEPA (LeCun, 2022; Assran et al.,
2023), LeJEPA (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025)), remain largely underexplored in the time-series
literature. Finally, while benchmarking libraries such as TSLib (Wang et al., 2024) provide valuable
standardization of downstream evaluation, they do not natively support controlled pre-training
workflows.

To address this gap, we empirically investigate the question:When Does Self-Supervised Learning
Transfer to Time-Series Tasks?Specifically, we formulate three guiding research questions:
• RQ1: When does SSL pre-training improve performance in time-series forecasting, clas-
sification, and anomaly detection over random frozen features (linear probing) or random
initialization (fine-tuning)?
• RQ2: Do certain SSL methods consistently outperform others across different downstream
tasks?
• RQ3: How do pre-training data source (real vs. synthetic), data scale, and backbone depth
affect representation transfer?
To answer these questions, we benchmark seven representative methods across five key SSL
paradigms: reconstruction, generative modeling, contrastive learning, invariance-based distillation,
and joint-embedding predictive architectures, alongside non-pre-trained controls. We evaluate models
pretrained on the real-world Monash repository (Godahewa et al., 2021), synthetic datasets (Taga
et al., 2025), and a mixture of the two, as well as in-domain pre-trained controls. To isolate the effect
of each SSL method, we keep the backbone architecture (a standard Transformer encoder), data splits,
training budgets, and evaluation protocols strictly identical across comparisons.
Our empirical results show that transfer efficacy depends on the downstream task and evaluation
protocol. SSL yields substantial gains in anomaly detection and provides effective initializations for
classification under fine-tuning, but offers limited to no advantage over non-pre-trained baselines in
forecasting. Furthermore, linear probing does not reliably predict fine-tuning outcomes. For example,
when pre-trained on Monash, LeJEPA achieves the highest mean accuracy on five out of nine
classification datasets under linear probing, but on only two after fine-tuning. Downstream rankings
in anomaly detection also vary substantially across evaluation metrics. In data and compute scaling,
scale-matched synthetic pre-training is often competitive with real-world corpora but increasing data
volume through synthetic generation does not improve performance. Finally, scaling Transformer
depth consistently degrades forecasting accuracy.
We distill our empirical findings into practical guidelines for time-series SSL, offering recommen-
dations for selecting pretext objectives, establishing rigorous baseline controls, and standardizing
downstream evaluation protocols.
2 Preliminaries
2.1 Self-Supervised Learning Paradigms
Self-supervised learning (SSL) aims to learn generalizable representations from unlabeled data by
optimizing proxy objectives designed around intrinsic structural priors (Chen et al., 2020). In this
work, we systematically evaluate five core SSL paradigms: reconstruction, generative modeling,
invariance-based distillation, contrastive learning, and joint-embedding predictive architectures
(JEPA), selecting representative methods for each (Table 1). Following Nie et al. (2023), we adopt a
channel-independent design in which each channel (variate) of a multivariate series x∈R T×C is
separately segmented intoL non-overlapping patches xi∈R P (T=L·P ). A shared Transformer
encoder fθ maps the patched series to latent representations z=f θ(x)∈R L×D, which are
processed by method-specific heads and serve as the backbone features for downstream adaptation.
Full architectural specifications and hyperparameters appear in Appendix A and C.4.

While for DINO and SoftCLT using native augmentations yields stable results, we often observe
collapse with LeJEPA. In order to support a fair comparison, we introduce an alternative augmentation
protocol for LeJEPA based on the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT). We decompose the input signal
into multi-scale approximation and detail coefficients and generate reference and perturbed views. A
reference view applies soft-thresholding to detail coefficients while preserving low-frequency trends,
whereas a perturbed view adds Gaussian noise. This encourages invariance to high-frequency sensor
noise while preserving underlying temporal morphology. Implementation details and comparisons
appear in Appendices A.5 and F.1.
2.2 Evaluating SSL Transferability
We formalize the evaluation protocol used to assess the transferability of learned representations.
Downstream Adaptation and Baseline Controls.We evaluate representations under three proto-
cols: linear probing, probing with a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), and full fine-tuning. In linear
and MLP probing, the pre-trained encoder backbone remains frozen while only a downstream task
head is trained, isolating the linear or non-linear accessibility of task-relevant features in the learned
latent space. Under full fine-tuning, the encoder and task head are updated jointly, assessing the
quality of the pre-trained weights as an optimization prior. In all cases, pre-training pretext heads are
discarded prior to downstream adaptation. To isolate the empirical transfer benefit attributable strictly
to self-supervised learning, every pre-trained model is paired with a matched random control, sharing
an identical backbone architecture, parameter count, and downstream optimization budget:
• Random Frozen Encoder (Probing Control):A randomly initialized, frozen backbone paired
with the same trained downstream head, measuring performance attainable solely from
architecture inductive priors.
• Supervised from Scratch (Fine-Tuning Control):An identical backbone randomly initialized
and trained end-to-end with the downstream task head under supervised optimization.
Quantifying SSL Transfer Gain.To directly measure the net performance gain of pre-training
relative to these baseline controls, we define theSSL transfer gain ∆ for a given taskT and adaptation
protocol:
∆(LSSL,T) =s T [M(θSSL,T)−M(θ baseline,T)],(8)
where M(θ,T) denotes the downstream evaluation metric, and θSSL and θbaseline represent the
pre-trained model and its corresponding control (θbaseline represents the frozen random encoder under
probing, or supervised-from-scratch training under fine-tuning). The direction scalar sT ∈{+1,−1}
aligns metric polarity: sT = +1 for higher-is-better metrics (e.g., accuracy) and sT =−1 for
lower-is-better error metrics (e.g., mean squared error). Consequently, ∆(LSSL,T)>0 strictly
indicates an empirical gain from pre-training over the baseline control.

3 Experimental Setup
Pre-Training and Evaluation Benchmarks.Our primary experiments pre-train models on the
Monash dataset (Godahewa et al., 2021), comprising approximately 680K time-series samples. To
isolate the effects of data origin and scale on downstream transfer quality, we also pre-train on scale-
matched synthetic data, as well as on a scaled-up corpus (2.5M samples) consisting of either purely
synthetic or mixed (Monash and synthetic) data. In addition, we evaluate an in-domain pre-training
control for forecasting tasks. Synthetic data generation follows TimePFN (Taga et al., 2025) and is
detailed in the Appendix C.1.
We evaluate on seven forecasting datasets (ETTh1/2, ETTm1/2 (Zhou et al., 2021), Weather, Electric-
ity, and Traffic (Wu et al., 2021)), nine UEA multivariate classification datasets (Bagnall et al., 2018),
and five anomaly-detection datasets (SMD (Su et al., 2019), PSM (Abdulaal et al., 2021), MSL and
SMAP (Hundman et al., 2018), and SWaT (Mathur & Tippenhauer, 2016)), following the splits and
evaluation protocols of TSLib (Wang et al., 2024).
Implementation Details.Unless otherwise specified, we use a shared 8-layer Transformer with
embedding dimension 128, 16 attention heads, context length 336, patch size 16, and 20 pre-training
epochs (full details in Appendix C.4). Main comparisons use method-specific learning-rate and
batch-size tuning with comparable tuning budgets, retaining required heads, views, and optimization
components. Appendix F.2 repeats the Monash comparison with identical learning rate, batch size,
epochs, and context. Results report means and standard deviations over five seeds unless specified
otherwise. Extended results and implementation details are provided in the appendix.

4.1 RQ1: When does SSL pre-training improve performance on downstream tasks?
Table 2 reports how often pre-trained SSL models outperform non-pre-trained baselines across linear
probing, MLP probing, and fine-tuning. Figure 1 details the dataset-level relative transfer gains
over the frozen random baseline under linear probing. Across tasks, downstream transfer varies:

forecasting exhibits minimal to negative transfer. Absolute scores and standard deviations appear in
Tables 10, 13, and 14 . We summarize the main trends per task below.
Anomaly detection.Anomaly detection shows the clearest transfer gain, with SSL outperforming
the random baseline in 54–94% of configurations under linear probing and up to 97% under MLP
probing (Table 2). Relative gains are highest on MSL and SMAP (Figure 1a), where models such
as JEPA and LeJEPA improve F1Pa by more than 250% over random features, driven by strong
lifts in both F1Af and F1Ev. However, these gains do not generalize to every benchmark: on SWaT,
every SSL method underperforms random features on F1Pa and F1Af, with only a marginal gain on
event-wise detection (e.g., NTP at 2.86% F1Ev).

4.2 RQ2: Do certain SSL methods consistently outperform others?
No single SSL method consistently outperforms the others. Instead, two main findings characterize
the empirical results:
(a) Under linear probing, performance splits by task target: when evaluating with a linear probe,
relative rankings depend on whether the downstream objective requires identifying discrete
semantic states (classification and segment-level detection) or tracking continuous numerical
trajectories (forecasting and point-wise residual scoring). LeJEPA achieves the highest
accuracy on five of nine classification datasets (Handwriting, UWave, FaceDetection, SCP1,
SpokenArabicDigits) and leads on MSL anomaly detection on both F1Pa and F1Af, where
identifying a coarse regime shift suffices. Yet on forecasting, it never beats a frozen random
encoder. A likely reason is that its regularizer (SIGReg) separates discrete classes well,
but discards the point-wise continuity needed to extrapolate trajectories (Appendix F.4.3).
Conversely, reconstruction and generative objectives perform better on continuous numerical
tasks: Diffusion leads on ETTh2, and NTP leads on ETTm1 and on SMD (F1Af). We also
note that for anomaly detection, SSL performance and relative ranking vary depending on
the evaluation metric (Fig. 2a).
(b) Linear probing does not predict MLP or fine-tuning performance: The ranking of SSL
models under linear probing changes substantially with non-linear probing (MLP) or when
unfreezing the encoder (Fig. 2b). In forecasting, Diffusion loses to a random encoder when
using a linear head, but achieves the lowest MSE on roughly 90% of datasets when evaluated
with a shallow MLP (with the remainder 10% within seed variance). The same trend is
observed for fine-tuning. After fine-tuning, LeJEPA retains its classification advantage on
only two datasets, as competing models close the performance gap in anomaly detection,
Diffusion trails the random baseline on PSM under linear probing (43.78% vs. 47.55%), but
beats every other SSL model after fine-tuning (48.30% vs. 28.29%). This suggests that while
strongly regularized embeddings yield competitive frozen features, other representations
serve as better starting points for fine-tuning.

4.3 RQ3: How Do Data Source, Scale, and Depth Affect Transfer?
We find that scaling pre-training data volume, training duration, and model depth does not yield
consistent gains. Instead, transfer efficacy depends heavily on the alignment between the pre-training
source and the downstream task structure.
Data source and domain relevance.Pairwise comparisons across all objective–dataset pairs show
that the best pre-training source depends on the task (Table 3). For forecasting, in-domain data
performs best, beating Monash and both synthetic sets. Compared to the scale-matched synthetic
dataset, Monash gives a slight advantage, but adding synthetic data to Monash hurts performance.
Mixing in synthetic data appears to wash out the real temporal patterns needed to forecast future
points.
For classification, this pattern flips. Scale-matched synthetic data beats Monash and large synthetic
data. A likely reason is that clean synthetic curves expose basic class shapes without adding irrelevant
sensor noise, making classes easier to separate. For anomaly detection, the preferred source depends
on the metric. Pretraining on Monash yields better F1Ev, compared to the matched-scale synthetic
dataset, and vice versa for F1Af.
Training duration and encoder depth.Extended pre-training yields negligible net benefits (Ta-
ble 4, top panel). Doubling pre-training from 20 to 40 epochs does not change classification and
degrades raw forecasting performance. While 40 epochs appears to favor anomaly detection, this
advantage vanishes once filtering out seed-level variance (≤0.005 MSE or≤1% accuracy/F1),
leaving mostly ties between the two training budgets.
Increasing the encoder depth degrades forecasting accuracy (Table 4, bottom panel). In a single-seed
sweep over N∈{2,4,8,12,24} layers, a 2-layer backbone outperforms an 8-layer encoder on
forecasting, while the 24-layer variant outperforms the 8-layer model in only 3 of 21 comparisons.
In classification, the 8-layer backbone similarly outperforms the 24-layer model. Under fixed
training budgets, shallower networks consistently match or exceed deep architectures, indicating that
parameter scaling practices from vision and NLP do not transfer directly to current time-series SSL
objectives, at least under the dataset scales investigated in our work.

6 Conclusion
We evaluated seven time-series SSL methods across forecasting, classification, and anomaly detection.
Downstream transfer varies by task and evaluation protocol, with clear benefits for anomaly detection,
selective gains for classification, and limited added value over non-pre-trained baselines in forecasting.
Linear probing does not reliably predict fine-tuning performance, and no single SSL method dominates
across all tasks. Furthermore, synthetic pre-training matches Monash on several benchmarks, whereas
scaling Transformer depth and pre-training duration does not improve performance.
Our findings are bounded by the evaluated Transformer backbones and pre-training dataset scales.
Key priorities for future work include evaluating scaling to larger multi-domain corpora, evaluating
on additional architectures and developing hybrid objectives that balance semantic invariance with
point-wise precision.

B Evaluation Baselines And Metrics
This section details how SSL transferability is evaluated: the adaptation protocols, the baseline
controls, and the metrics. For all tasks, we follow the dataset splits and evaluation protocols of
TSLib (Wang et al., 2024).
B.1 Protocols and Baselines
Protocols.We assess the learned representations under three adaptation protocols, which differ in
the capacity of the task head and in whether the encoder is updated:
16

=== PAGE BREAK ===

• Linear probing.The pre-trained backbone is frozen and a single linear head is trained for
each downstream task.
• MLP probing.The pre-trained backbone is frozen and a shallow MLP head (one hidden
layer of width 512, GELU activation, dropout 0.2) is trained for each downstream task.
• Full fine-tuning.The pre-trained backbone and the task head are trained jointly on the
downstream task.
Each protocol isolates a different aspect of transfer. Linear probing measures whether task-relevant
information is linearly accessible in the frozen latent space, so that performance reflects the structure
of the representation rather than the capacity of a non-linear head to compensate for it. MLP probing
measures whether that information is present but only non-linearly accessible. Full fine-tuning
measures the quality of the pre-trained weights as an initialization for task-specific optimization.
Baselines.To quantify the benefit of self-supervised pre-training, every pre-trained model is
compared against a non-pre-trained control with an identical Transformer backbone, parameter
count, and downstream training budget. Under linear and MLP probing, the control is a randomly
initialized, frozen encoder; under full fine-tuning, it is the same architecture trained from scratch on
the downstream task. We regard pre-training as beneficial only when it outperforms the corresponding
control, which is the condition ∆>0 in Eq. 8. These controls are strong rather than nominal: simple
or untrained models have been shown to match more elaborate deep architectures on downstream
time-series tasks (Zeng et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2022).

Setup.We consider anomaly detection in multivariate time series, where timestamp t is flagged
as anomalous, ˆyt = 1, when its anomaly score st (Eq. 18) exceeds a threshold τ. Following the
TSLib (Wang et al., 2024) protocol, τ is the (100−r) -th percentile of the pooled train-and-test
score distribution, wherer is the benchmark’s anomaly ratio (0.5% for SMD and 1.0% for all other
datasets); no validation-based tuning is performed. Letyt∈{0,1} denote the ground-truth label and
E={E 1,...,E |E|} the set of ground-truth anomaly events, i.e., the maximal contiguous runs of
anomalous timestamps. Because point adjustment is known to inflate even weak detectors (Kim et al.,
2022; Wu & Keogh, 2023), we report three complementary scores. All three are computed from the
same binary sequence{ˆyt} and take the form F1 = 2PR/(P+R) ; they differ only in how precision
P and recallR are defined. Unless stated otherwise, reported values are percentages averaged over
five seeds.
Metrics.Here we define the three anomaly detection metrics reported:
• Point-adjusted F1 (F1Pa): if any timestamp within an eventEk is flagged, all timestamps
inEk are counted as detected; precision, recall, and F1 are then computed point-wise over
the adjusted predictions. This is the standard protocol of TSLib (Wang et al., 2024) and
prior work, but is lenient toward sparse detections inside long events.
• Event-wise F1 (F1Ev): each ground-truth event counts as a single positive and each maximal
run of predicted anomalies as a single detection. An event is recalled if at least one prediction
falls within it; a predicted run is a true positive if it overlaps at least one event. Precision
and recall are computed over events and detections rather than timestamps, so a long event
contributes once regardless of its length.
• Affiliation F1 (F1Af)(Huet et al., 2022): each timestamp is affiliated to its nearest ground-
truth event, partitioning the time axis into affiliation zones. Within each zone, precision is
the average distance from predicted anomalies to the event and recall is the average distance
from event timestamps to the nearest prediction, both converted to probabilities against
a uniformly random predictor over that zone and averaged across events. Unlike point
adjusted and event-wise metrics, this metric is distance-based, parameter-free, and penalizes
both early/late detections and false alarms far from any event.
C Experimental Setup
This section details the experimental setup: the pre-training corpora, the evaluation datasets, the
implementation and hyperparameter details.
C.1 Pre-training Datasets
To isolate the effect of pre-training data source and scale on downstream tasks, we pre-train every
objective on the five datasets. Dataset sizes are counted in training windows of length (336).
• Monash (680K):the real-world Monash corpus. It is the reference corpus for all main-text
results and measures transfer from observed, multi-domain data.
• Synthetic (680K):a purely synthetic corpus matched to Monash in size. Comparing it with
Monash isolates the effect of data source at fixed scale.
• Synthetic (2.5M):a larger draw from the same synthetic generator. Comparing it with
Synthetic (680K) isolates the effect of scale at fixed source.

• Hybrid (2.5M):the full Monash corpus supplemented with ∼1.8M synthetic windows to
match the size of Synthetic (2.5M).
• In-domain:the training data of the target forecasting benchmark itself. It is evaluated for
forecasting only.
Monash.We use the Monash Time Series Forecasting Repository (Godahewa et al., 2021), a
collection spanning diverse domains including finance, weather, and energy. The pre-training set
consists of 683,982 training windows, with 77,365 windows held out for validation. It represents a
realistic yet relatively small-scale pre-training scenario.
In-domain.For in-domain pre-training, each forecasting benchmark’s own training and validation
splits serve as the pre-training dataset, so that the encoder is pre-trained and evaluated on the same
dataset. The test split is never used for pre-training.
Synthetic.To study the effect of scale in the absence of domain bias, we generate synthetic data
following TimePFN (Taga et al., 2025). We create two datasets, of 680,000 and 2,512,000 training
windows, respectively. These series provide diverse temporal “building blocks” such as trends and
periodicities, but may lack the stochastic variability of real-world data. The generation procedure is
detailed below.
Hybrid.The hybrid corpus combines the full Monash training set with ∼1.8M synthetic windows,
for a total of 2.5M. It supports two comparisons. Against Synthetic (2.5M), which has the same size,
it isolates the effect of replacing part of the synthetic data with real data. Against Monash (680K),
which it contains, it isolates the effect of adding synthetic volume to a fixed set of real data.
C.2 Synthetic Data Generation
The synthetic corpus follows the structural-prior generation scheme introduced in TimePFN (Taga
et al., 2025), which we instantiate using the original scripts with adjusted sequence length and dataset
size. Each univariate time series is sampled as a single realization from a Gaussian Process (GP)
prior on a uniform temporal grid of length T , where the covariance is constructed by randomly
composing base kernels drawn (with replacement) from a fixed bank. The number of kernels per
series is sampled uniformly (K∼U{1,...,5} ), and kernels are combined via random addition or
multiplication operators (Bernoulli( 1
2)), applied in a left-folded manner.
The kernel bank includes periodic (ExpSineSquared) kernels spanning multiple temporal resolutions
(e.g., daily, weekly, yearly), smooth kernels (RBF), multi-scale kernels (Rational Quadratic), linear
kernels (Dot-Product), and noise components, enabling diverse temporal patterns such as trends,
seasonality, and irregular fluctuations.
Multivariate series are generated using a Linear Coregionalization Model (LCM). A set of latent
univariate GP factors is first sampled, and each channel is constructed as a simplex-weighted
combination of these latent processes:

This construction yields synthetic data with controlled temporal structure and inter-channel dependen-

real benchmarks and represents a known limitation of the generator. We refer to Taga et al. (2025) for
the full generation algorithm and parameterization.

C.4 Implementation And Training Details
All methods share the Transformer encoder backbone of Section 2.1. The default configuration has 8
layers, embedding dimensionD= 128 , 16 attention heads, feed-forward dimension 512, and patch
lengthP= 16 . The input context is 336 timesteps (L= 21 patches) for pre-training, forecasting,
and anomaly detection, and 1,152 timesteps (L= 72 patches) for classification. Method-specific
components are added only where the SSL objective requires them: a linear reconstruction decoder
for MAE, a residual MLP prediction head for NTP, a cross-attention denoising decoder for Diffusion,
a projection head and an EMA teacher for DINO, and a latent predictor and an EMA teacher for JEPA;
LeJEPA and SoftCLT use the encoder alone. All such components are discarded after pre-training, so
downstream evaluation uses the encoder only.
Pre-Training DetailsAll objectives are pre-trained for 20 epochs on windows of 336 timesteps
with patch lengthP= 16 . Each objective constructs its training signal as described in Appendix F.2:
masking for MAE and JEPA, causal prediction for NTP, noise corruption for Diffusion, and augmented
views for DINO, SoftCLT, and LeJEPA. Learning rate and batch size are tuned per objective under
comparable tuning budgets, and all objective-specific hyperparameters are listed in Table 9; the
matched-budget comparison of Appendix F.2 instead fixes them across objectives. We saw our
experiments, JEPA collapsed to a constant representation under both Adam and AdamW, whereas
SGD trained stably. For every run, we keep the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss, and all
pre-training runs are repeated over five seeds.
Downstream Training DetailsFor classification and anomaly detection, downstream hyperpa-
rameters are shared across all SSL objectives and the non-pre-trained controls, so that differences
in the result tables reflect the encoder weights rather than the adaptation budget. Classification uses
a learning rate of 1e−3 with a batch size of 64 for 20 epochs. Anomaly detection uses Adam with
a learning rate of 1e−3 and cosine annealing for 10 epochs, with early stopping at a patience of 3.
Forecasting uses dataset-specific batch sizes (16–256) and learning rates in [1e−4,4e−4] , and is
trained for 20 epochs.
C.5 Compute and Train-time Details
All experiments were conducted on NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs (96GB VRAM, 600W
TDP), with each backbone trained on a single GPU. Peak memory usage during pre-training ranged
between approximately 8–32GB depending on model depth and dataset. On the Monash collection,
pre-training runtime scaled with backbone depth: 2–4 layer models required approximately 2–3
hours, 8-layer models 4–5 hours, 12-layer models 6–7 hours, and 24-layer models around 10 hours
per run. Synthetic and mixed-data variants approximately doubled these runtimes, with the deepest
24-layer configurations requiring up to 28 hours.
Downstream evaluation is substantially lighter than pre-training. Classification completes within
approximately 20 minutes per dataset, and anomaly detection within 15 minutes per dataset, depending
on model depth. Forecasting on smaller benchmarks (ETTm1/2, ETTh1/2, Weather) typically
completes within 15 minutes per dataset, while larger datasets scale more significantly: Electricity
requires up to 4 hours and Traffic up to 12 hours for the deepest configurations. Memory usage during
downstream tasks remains below 20GB for most datasets, increasing to approximately 40GB for
Electricity, while certain Traffic configurations may exceed available VRAM (out-of-memory).

Forecasting.Table 15 reports the forecasting results. Diffusion is the only objective with consistent
gains: it outperforms the random frozen encoder on all five datasets under both MSE and MAE, and
it attains the lowest MSE on four of five datasets and the lowest MAE on all five (tied with DINO on
ETTm2). Its only loss in MSE is to DINO onETTm2, by 0.001 ( 0.261±0.001 vs. 0.260±0.002 ),
which is within seed variance. Since Diffusion beats the random encoder on only two of these
five datasets under linear probing, this suggests that its representations encode forecasting-relevant
information that is not linearly accessible and requires a non-linear head. The remaining objectives
do not show consistent gains: four of seven improve onWeather, and DINO leads onETTm2, but
overall the pre-trained encoders outperform the control in only 11 of 35 comparisons, five of which
are due to Diffusion.
Classification.Table 16 reports the classification results. The number of wins over the control
drops relative to linear probing, from 29 to 25 of 63 objective–dataset pairs. This drop reflects a
stronger control rather than weaker pre-trained encoders: with the MLP head, the random frozen
encoder improves on eight of nine datasets, most strongly onHeartbeat(from 66.63 to 73.37). On
that dataset, NTP, DINO, and SoftCLT exceed the control under linear probing but fall just below it
with the MLP head (73.05, 72.56, and 73.29 vs. 73.37), and four of the eight lost wins are by less
than 0.35 points, within seed variance. Though we do see that LeJEPA attains the best accuracy on
four datasets, with gains of ∆ = +41.2 onUWave(82.97 vs. 41.81), +15.2 onSpokenArabicDigits,
and +14.7 onHandwriting. This suggests that a non-linear head recovers from random features part
of the class information that pre-training makes linearly accessible, which narrows the margin on the
datasets where the advantage of pre-training was small.
Anomaly detection.Table 17 reports the anomaly-detection results. The MLP head supports better
results for the pre-trained encoders: the wins under F1PA, F1Ev, and F1Af rise to 26, 34, and 29 of

unchanged for NTP (13 of 15). For the random encoder the picture is mixed, as it gains on 8 of the
15 dataset–metric cells and drops on 7. This suggests that the MLP head is appropriate for this task,
strengthening the pre-trained encoders relative to the baseline.

This section gives the extended results for Table 3.
D.2.1 Overview
Figure 7 summarizes performance under Monash, Monash-sized synthetic, Large Synthetic, In-
domain and Hybrid pre-training. Because Monash contains approximately 680K samples, whereas
Large Synthetic and Hybrid contain 2.5M samples, we use the matched-scale comparisons to separate
data source from corpus size. Throughout these experiments, the effect of data composition varies
by task. At matched scale, Monash-sized synthetic pre-training is often competitive with Monash
pre-training, although the two are not uniformly equivalent. At the larger 2.5M-sample scale, Large
Synthetic and Hybrid pre-training also produce similar results for many objectives, suggesting limited
benefit from adding the evaluated real-data component in those settings. For example, LeJEPA on
UWaveobtains an accuracy of 63.3% with Large Synthetic pre-training, 71.2% with Monash-sized
synthetic pre-training, and 71.69% with Monash pre-training. Overall, these results support synthetic
data as a useful pre-training source within the evaluated regime, but do not establish that transfer
is independent of data origin: the objective–dataset exceptions above show that source and scale
interact.

Across all four sources, JEPA, LeJEPA, and Diffusion lead the spacecraft-telemetry datasets. All

E.1 Extended Results for 40 Epoch Evaluation
To test whether the per-model results are limited by pre-training budget, we double the schedule
from 20 to 40 epochs while holding every other hyper-parameter at its native per-model value: the
same 8-layer encoder, 336-step context, patch length, optimiser, and learning rate, so the only change
is twice as many parameter updates. All backbones are pre-trained on Monash and evaluated by
linear probing over 5 seeds. We run the same experiment with a 24-layered encoder on single
seed. Tables 31, 32, 33, and 34 report classification, anomaly detection, forecasting, and 24 layer
classification. We also report results of 24 layered transformer with 40 epochs
Results.Doubling the pre-training budget leaves the 20-epoch conclusions intact. Rankings are
unchanged across all three task families: LeJEPA still leads the gesture and morphology benchmarks,
signal-space objectives the periodic ones, JEPA and LeJEPA the spacecraft-telemetry anomaly
datasets, and the Random frozen encoder baseline still attains the lowest forecasting MSE on
ETTh1, Electricity, and Traffic. Most cells move by less than one accuracy point or 0.005 MSE.
SoftCLT is the exception. SoftCLT improves forecasting on every dataset (ETTh10.456→0.442 ,
Electricity 0.217→0.195 , Traffic 0.488→0.431 ), but remains above the random encoder on six of
seven, matching it only on Traffic (0.431 vs. 0.435). The additional updates otherwise amplify the
failure modes already visible at 20 epochs: JEPA collapses on JapaneseV owels, SpokenArabicDigits,
and UWave with seed standard deviations of 8–22 points, and LeJEPA’s forecasting degradation
deepens on every dataset (Traffic 0.848→0.940 ), consistent with the isotropy induced by SIGReg
accumulating with training. The forecasting transfer gain does not emerge with more training, so the
gap to the random encoder is not a symptom of under-training, in line with the convergence analysis
in Appendix E.2.

E.2 Depth Scaling
Downstream performance across depthsFigure 8 shows forecasting performance as a function
of Transformer depth. Across objectives, MSE is approximately flat between 2 and 8 layers and
often increases at greater depths; the shallowest configuration is frequently within approximately 0.01
MSE of the best result. The rise beyond 8 layers is particularly visible for MAE onETTh1(0.438
atN=8 vs. 0.554 atN=24) and SoftCLT onETTm1(0.375 at N=8 vs. 0.692 atN=12); these are
single-seed sweep values and differ from the five-seed the main tables. The broad conclusion that
SSL provides limited forecasting benefit remains stable across depths. Although the deeper models
train stably under the fixed budget (Appendix E.2), this experiment is not compute-normalized and
does not isolate whether degradation at greater depth arises from overfitting, optimization efficiency,
or objective–capacity interactions.
Implications for Depth Scaling.These results support our main finding that the absence of
forecasting gains beyond eight layers is not caused by insufficient training. Even at 24 layers, models
converge within the allocated budget, suggesting that the lack of improvement at greater depth
reflects the learned representations rather than optimization constraints. While we do not perform
compute-normalized scaling (e.g., matched optimization steps), the consistent convergence behavior
across depths indicates that additional training would likely yield only marginal improvements.
Finally, deeper models incur substantially higher computational cost. For example, 24-layer models
trained on Synthetic or Hybrid data require up to 28 hours per run (Appendix C.5), reinforcing the
practical relevance of the depth result.

E.3 Convergence Analysis
Convergence Under Fixed Pre-training Budget.All models are pre-trained for a fixed budget of
20 epochs across all backbone depths, including the deepest 24-layer configurations. Empirically, we
observe stable convergence behavior across all evaluated SSL paradigms. Training loss decreases
consistently in early epochs and plateaus thereafter, with no evidence of instability or divergence
even for deep models.
Model selection is based on the best validation checkpoint rather than the final epoch. Across methods,
peak validation performance typically occurs before the full budget is exhausted, most commonly
around epochs 15–16. This indicates that the fixed 20-epoch budget is sufficient for convergence and
does not disadvantage deeper models.

F Controlled Ablations
F.1 Augmentations Ablation Study LeJEPA
Setup.To isolate the impact of augmentation design on LeJEPA, we evaluate several families of
transformations beyond the default DWT-based pipeline. Each augmentation is applied symmetrically
to construct dual views and is parameterized by a single magnitude controlling the strength of the
transformation. We consider the following families:
• Galilean & Special-Relativistic:global temporal rescaling and local Lorentz-style transfor-
mations of the formγ(x−v·t).
• Coordinate Transformations:global polar warps and local rotations in the value–time
plane.
• Hyperbolic Transformations:global hyperbolic warps and local Möbius shifts on the
Poincaré disk.
• DWT (baseline):wavelet-based augmentations with structured perturbations in the fre-
quency domain.
Results.Across the forecasting benchmarks, DWT-based augmentations consistently provide the
most stable and competitive performance.
LeJEPAForforecasting, DWT achieves on par with hyperbolic, (lowest MSE on ETTM1, lowest
MAE on ETTM2, and ETTH1), on par with hyperbolic, and passing the others. while onclassification,
LeJEPA wins 5 out of 9, winning them by a large margin, while on the other 4, DWT is highly
competitive.
Discussion.These results indicate that the effectiveness of augmentations depends strongly on
the alignment between the induced invariances and the underlying signal structure. DWT-based
augmentations preserve temporal locality while introducing structured perturbations in the frequency
domain, which appears well-suited for forecasting.
In contrast, the alternative transformations introduce more global or nonlinear distortions that are
less aligned with the statistical properties of real-world time series. While these transformations are
theoretically motivated, they do not translate into effective inductive biases in practice.
Conclusion.Overall, the ablation confirms that the choice of augmentation is critical for LeJEPA.
DWT-based augmentations provide a robust and well-aligned inductive bias, while alternative trans-
formation families fail to consistently improve performance. This justifies our design choice of using
wavelet-based augmentations as the default across all experiments.

F.4 Representation Analyses
F.4.1 Embedding Analysis
We provide a qualitative analysis of the learned representations using a t-SNE visualization on the
UWaveGestureLibrarydataset (Figure 10). Each point corresponds to an embedded time series
instance, colored by its class label.
Since this dataset is used exclusively for classification, the visualization reflects the degree ofclass
separabilityinduced by each SSL paradigm. Clear differences emerge between methods. DINO
produces well-separated and compact clusters, indicating strong discriminative structure in the
embedding space. LeJEPA also yields highly separable clusters, though with slightly more dispersion,
suggesting that its invariance constraints preserve class-level structure while allowing some intra-class
variability. JEPA forms coherent but less sharply separated clusters, reflecting a softer organization of
the latent space.
In contrast,Signal-space predictive and reconstructivemethods exhibit weaker class separation.
MAE produces partially clustered representations with noticeable overlap between classes, while
NTP shows more entangled embeddings with limited global organization. Diffusion lies between
these extremes, forming moderately structured but less compact clusters compared to alignment-based
approaches.
Overall, the visualization is consistent with the classification results. LeJEPA’s separable clusters
translate directly into top linear-probing accuracy. DINO’s compact clusters indicate that class
structure is present in the embedding, yet its linear-probing accuracy on this dataset is low; since
t-SNE is non-linear, this structure is not linearly accessible from the frozen representation, which is
consistent with DINO’s recovery under full fine-tuning (Section 4).

F.4.2 Temporal Latent Drift
To probe how the learned representations respond to temporal perturbations, we measure the displace-
ment of frozen encoder outputs under two families of input perturbation. For each objective we load
the Monash-pre-trained backbone (seed 1337, context 336) and embed 300 non-overlapping windows
from each of Weather and ETTh1 (z-scored per channel, stride 48). Each window is perturbed either
by a circular time-shift ofk∈{1,2,4,8,16,32} steps or by zeroing a randomp∈{10%,20%} of
its timesteps, and re-embedded. Drift is the cosine distanceD= 1−cos(z,z ε) between the clean
and perturbed embeddings, averaged over windows and datasets. We mean-pool the patch tokens
before computing D; concatenating tokens instead would register a whole-patch shift as a large
change purely because tokens move to different slots, which conflates re-ordering with a change in
content. Results are from a single pre-training seed and carry no downstream label; they describe
representation geometry only.

Findings.Two patterns emerge (Table 40, Figure 11). First, whole-patch shifts separate the
objectives into two groups. MAE, Diffusion, JEPA, and SoftCLT return to near-zero drift atk=16:
after pooling, their representation is largely a function of which patch contents are present, not
where they occur. NTP does not ( 0.023→0.030 ), consistent with its causal mask making token
representations position-dependent by construction. DINO and LeJEPA show no such dip and drift
monotonically with shift, so their tokens depend on absolute position and the pooled embedding
describes the window as a whole. This is a direct geometric counterpart of the distinction drawn
in Section 5: the reconstructive and denoising objectives yield local, content-based representations,
while the two view-based objectives yield holistic ones. Second, latent-space alignment encoders are
moredisplaced by local masking than signal-space predictive and reconstructive ones (LeJEPA 0.16,
JEPA 0.48 vs. NTP and MAE≈0.02). We caution against reading this as a lack of robustness: cosine
drift in a near-isotropic embedding (Appendix A.5) registers any change, whereas in an embedding
dominated by a few high-variance directions most input changes are invisible to cosine distance even
when a linear head can exploit them. The probe therefore characterises what the representations
encode, not how much fine-grained information they retain; it does not by itself establish the precision
side of the precision–invariance hypothesis.

These observations motivate the hypothesis that the geometry encouraged by SIGReg interacts
differently with classification and forecasting. They do not establish isotropy as the cause of the
forecasting degradation. Removing SIGReg produces a collapsed representation, so that ablation does

that vary representation geometry while preserving non-collapsed solutions and otherwise comparable
training conditions.
