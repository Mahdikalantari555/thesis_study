Abstract
Efficient crop health monitoring is crucial for global food security. Supervised deep learn-
ing approaches are often impractical due to the scarcity of large, labeled datasets. To
address this limitation, this study adapts EfficientAD, an unsupervised, label-free anomaly
detection framework originally designed for industrial inspection, for agricultural imagery
on small datasets. The method utilizes a Patch Description Network (PDN) for localized
feature extraction, a student network for local anomalies, and an autoencoder for global
structural constraints. Benchmarked against AnoGAN, Pix2Pix, InTra, and Teacher–Student
models, the framework demonstrated superior performance on the MVTec AD, PlantVil-
lage, Coffee Leaf, and a custom real-world Sweet Potato dataset. The model achieved
perfect area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) scores of up to
100% in categories like “Pongamia”, “Potato”, and “Coffee Leaf”. While image-level clas-
sification was exceptionally robust, pixel-level localization (AUPRO) proved sensitive to
complex agricultural backgrounds. To overcome this, a background interference analysis
was conducted using Background Removed (BGRM) and out-of-distribution Background
Replaced-Green (BGRP-G) strategies on the custom dataset. Notably, the BGRP-G strategy
remarkably improved the image-level AUROC from 88.9% to 99.5% and substantially
boosted the pixel-level AUPRO from 47.1% to 61.9%, successfully preserving the boundary
integrity of severe structural defects. Achieving millisecond-level latency without complex
data augmentation, this adapted label-free framework offers a versatile, highly efficient
solution for real-time crop health diagnostics on resource-constrained Edge AI devices.
Keywords: anomaly detection; agricultural imagery; knowledge distillation; unsupervised
learning; defect localization

efficiency have emerged as critical issues, posing considerable challenges for crop disease
detection and health management. Conventional agricultural anomaly detection involves
human experts performing manual visual inspections and is affected by various limitations,

These techniques leverage image processing and machine learning algorithms for rapid
and accurate identification of crop anomalies. These techniques reduce reliance on manual

and challenging to acquire in the agricultural domain [4,5]. This difficulty is exacerbated
by the heterogeneity and diversity of crop diseases. To mitigate the high data demands of
supervised learning, this study developed models optimized for small datasets. The study
used simplified model architectures optimized in terms of feasibility and scalability for
the practical application of image-based anomaly detection under resource-constrained
conditions. The proposed approach sought to achieve detection accuracy comparable
to models trained on large datasets and provide effective solutions to the diversity and
uncertainty inherent in agricultural anomaly detection.
This study leveraged label-free image-based anomaly detection technologies from
the industrial domain. Trained with transfer learning, the proposed model comprises
the EfficientAD framework, which possesses robust feature extraction capabilities via a
distilled Patch Description Network and high detection throughput, and enables the precise
identification of subtle anomalies across various image types. Accordingly, the framework
is well-suited for addressing the challenges posed by agricultural imagery, including the
diversity of crop species, variability in environmental conditions, and heterogeneity of
disease characteristics. Moreover, this study collected image data from field-grown sweet
potatoes to validate the practical applicability of the model in actual field environments.
This allowed for evaluating the model’s detection and localization performance under
non-laboratory conditions, thereby assessing and extending the real-world value of the
proposed detection methodology.
The objectives of this study were as follows:
1. Adapt and Apply Label-Free Anomaly Detection Models: Based on technologically
mature and advanced models used in industrial settings, anomaly detection algorithms
were adapted and tailored to agricultural imagery.
2. Enhance Detection Accuracy: Transfer learning techniques were refined and tested
to improve the accuracy and processing speed of detection, enabling the rapid and
precise identification and localization of crop anomalies.
3. Compare Across Diverse Datasets and Models: The proposed models were trained
and tested on multiple publicly available standard datasets. The performance and
generalization capabilities of the proposed models were compared with other anomaly
detection methods.
4. Validate Real-World Applicability: The image data from field-grown sweet potatoes were
collected to assess the model’s suitability and feasibility in actual field environments.

and pesticides, the cultivation of superior crop varieties, advancements in irrigation sys-

has enabled agricultural monitoring systems to perform real-time tracking of critical pa-

In addition, artificial intelligence and big data analytics have facilitated the processing and
analysis of extensive agricultural datasets, providing predictive and targeted recommenda-

The integration of precise data, convenient access to information, efficient resource
management, and intelligent decision-support systems has greatly improved agricultural
production efficiency. The evolution of pest and disease detection from conventional meth-
ods to artificial intelligence-driven technologies has brought substantial advancements
in identification accuracy, further improving detection efficiency and strengthening the
capacity to manage crop diseases effectively. Conventional approaches to defect identifi-
cation rely on visual inspections by experts and basic biochemical tests. These methods
are heavily dependent on human expertise, requiring plant symptoms to be assessed by
experts or farmers through direct observation. Such approaches are labor-intensive and
inefficient when applied to large-scale operations [8].
With the development of computer vision technologies, digital image analysis and
spectral analysis have emerged as innovative tools for agricultural defect detection. Digital
image analysis employs computational techniques to process plant images, extracting
anomalous features for evaluation. Spectral analysis uses data from various wavelengths
to assess plant health, considerably enhancing accuracy and efficiency in defect detection
by reducing reliance on visual inspection. Several studies have found that using extra
indices enhances the accuracy in identification [9] and regression [10] tasks. While data
accumulation and computational capacity continue to improve, machine learning has
assumed a pivotal role in the identification of agricultural pests and diseases. Machine
learning algorithms offer data-driven solutions for pest and disease classification. Artificial
neural networks, inspired by biological neural networks, enable models to detect complex
defect features, thereby improving identification precision [ 8]. For instance, the graph
pyramid attention network achieved nearly 99.0% supervised classification accuracy on
multiple agricultural datasets [ 11]. Furthermore, transfer learning and object detection
methods, including YOLO and Faster R-CNN, have been widely applied to agricultural
defect detection, facilitating simultaneous localization and classification while maintaining
robust performance on small datasets [8,12–14].
Deep learning presents a powerful alternative for agricultural defect detection, sup-
ported by established convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures widely ap-
plied in tasks like leaf and tuber disease classification (e.g., EfficientNet and ResNet
variants) [15–18]. In the industrial domain, unsupervised, label-free anomaly detection has
achieved remarkable success by learning exclusively from defect-free samples. However,
its adoption in agriculture remains limited. Unlike standardized manufactured goods,
agricultural products exhibit massive intra-class variance due to natural growth, making
it highly challenging to establish a strict, pure “normal” baseline without inadvertently
teaching the model to flag natural biological variations as anomalies.
Anomaly detection and localization techniques predominantly rely on unsupervised
methods that do not require large volumes of annotated anomaly data. Agricultural
image anomaly detection models can be broadly categorized into three types: conventional
image processing models, generative adversarial network (GAN) models, and feature
extraction-based models.
Conventional image processing techniques, such as edge detection and image en-
hancement, identify defects by analyzing edge features or color differences in images [19].
Although these methods are straightforward and intuitive, they face challenges in ad-

synthetic images to mitigate the need for extensive manual annotation and address the

dealing with the diverse and unpredictable characteristics of real-world defects. Feature

Artificial neural networks are widely applied in computer vision algorithms and have
made significant contributions to models used in plant disease detection and crop classifica-

In the agricultural domain, defect detection models often focus on identifying a single
species, with subsequent refinements enhancing their accuracy and efficiency for specific
crops. GANs and image enhancement techniques are predominantly used to address the
scarcity of image data in agriculture. Lu et al. [ 20] reviewed the applications of GANs

precision livestock management, and highlighted the effectiveness of GANs in generating
synthetic images to increase training sample sizes and enhance detection accuracy. Zhang
et al. [21] employed a convolutional neural network to detect defects in pears and integrated
a GAN for data augmentation to improve model accuracy.
In crop health monitoring, GAN variants such as conditional GAN and deep convolu-
tional GAN are widely applied to augment image datasets, effectively improving disease
detection accuracy. Hu et al. [22] used a conditional deep convolutional GAN to enhance
tea leaf disease images, considerably increasing the accuracy of the VGG16 model. Cycle-
GAN demonstrated strong image transformation capabilities across agricultural imagery
of various species, generating images of diseased and healthy crops and enhancing disease
detection accuracy [23].
Anomaly detection techniques are divided into three types based on whether they
involve reconstruction, synthetic anomalies, or feature representation. Reconstruction
techniques use autoencoders or GANs to reconstruct input images, detecting anomalies
through reconstruction errors. The models are trained to fully reconstruct normal samples;
when presented with anomalous samples, the generator fails to effectively reconstruct
the abnormal regions, leading to larger reconstruction errors, which are used for anomaly
detection. Synthetic anomaly techniques address the imbalance between anomalous and
normal samples by generating synthetic anomalous images to supplement the dataset.
Feature representation techniques leverage the powerful feature extraction capabilities of
deep learning models to identify anomalies in the feature space rather than at the image
level. Unlike the previous two techniques, they rely on deep feature extraction to determine
sample anomalies.
Examples of reconstruction techniques are AE-SSIM [24] and RIAD [25]. Examples
of synthetic anomaly techniques are Draem [26] and CutPaste [27]. Examples of feature
representation techniques are CS-Flow [28], PaDiM [29], and PatchCore [30]. The Inpainting
Transformer (InTra) technique [31], which was developed for anomaly detection, uses image
inpainting and self-attention mechanisms. AnoGAN [32] is a synthetic anomaly method
that trains on normal samples and evaluates anomalies by calculating reconstruction errors
and discriminator feature discrepancies. Pix2Pix GAN [ 33] detects anomalies through
image transformation and color-based reconstruction techniques. The Teacher–Student
model [34] is a feature representation method that employs a teacher model to learn the
distribution of normal sample features and a student model to replicate the teacher’s
outputs, identifying anomalies by comparing the discrepancies between their outputs.
Each anomaly detection technique offers distinct advantages. Reconstruction methods
are well-suited for scenarios with scarce anomalous samples. Synthetic anomaly methods

3. Materials and Methods
3.1. Dataset
This study used four datasets—MVTec AD [ 24,36], PlantVillage [ 37–39], Plant
Leaves [40,41], and the Coffee Leaf Disease Dataset [42,43]—as sources for model training
and testing (Table 1). While the full MVTec AD dataset was utilized to validate the funda-
mental architectural soundness of the model against standard industrial benchmarks, this
study strategically selected representative subsets from the PlantVillage and Coffee Leaf
datasets. This selective approach aims to test the “small dataset” hypothesis across distinct
morphological challenges (e.g., distinct localized spots vs. blurry gradual lesions) without
redundant computation, which is highly relevant for edge-AI development.
The MVTec AD dataset contains 5354 images divided into five texture types and
10 object categories. Each category includes defect-free training images and both defective
and defect-free testing images, enabling models to learn to distinguish between normal
and anomalous patterns. The MVTec AD dataset is frequently used as a benchmark for
evaluating performance in anomaly detection tasks.
To comprehensively evaluate the model’s generalization capabilities across diverse
leaf morphologies, we constructed a combined Plant Leaf evaluation pool by strategically
sampling from two distinct public repositories. The majority of the agricultural crop
classes (such as Apple, Cherry, Grape, and Potato) were sampled from the well-established
PlantVillage dataset [37–39] (Data for: Identification of Plant Leaf Diseases Using a 9-layer
Deep Convolutional Neural Network). It consists of 54,303 high-resolution images of plant
leaves from 14 crop species that have been divided into 38 health status categories. The
dataset encompasses a wide variety of plant disease types, supporting the generalization
capabilities of machine learning models across different plant diseases. However, to
introduce more complex structural variations and test the model’s robustness on medicinal
plants, we supplemented this pool with the Arjun and Pongamia classes. These were
sampled from a separate repository: A Database of Leaf Images: Practice towards Plant
Conservation with Plant Pathology [40,41] (indexed as plant_leaves in TensorFlow datasets).
By merging these standard crops and extended medicinal leaf datasets, we created a
rigorous, multi-species testbed (referred to collectively under the PlantVillage umbrella
in our results for brevity). To ensure strict evaluation protocols aligned with our “small
dataset” objective, only a highly constrained subset of images was selected for each class
(see Table 1), and testing sets were completely sequestered during the feature extraction
and model training phases.

3.2. Method Architecture
This study employed the EfficientAD framework (Figure 1) [35] to perform agricultural
disease image detection. Integrating student–teacher learning and autoencoder–based
techniques, the framework enables effective and precise agricultural imagery anomaly
detection with millisecond-level latency. The framework consists of three main components:
a Patch Description Network (PDN) for feature extraction, a student–teacher pair for local
anomaly detection, and an autoencoder for logical anomaly detection.
Figure 1. Architecture of the Patch Description Network (PDN) for lightweight feature extraction
(redrawn from [35]).
3.2.1. Feature Extraction
Instead of a heavy backbone, the framework utilizes a Patch Description Network
(PDN) for efficient feature extraction. The PDN, distilled from a pretrainedWideResNet-101
on the ImageNet dataset, was specifically chosen over other architectures like Vision Trans-
formers (ViTs) or EfficientNet because its convolutional structure excels at extracting dense,
localized, and low-level texture descriptors. While ViTs focus on global context—which
may overlook fine-grained local anomalies, the WideResNet-based PDN maintains a strict
and narrow receptive field (e.g., 33× 33 pixels). This property is crucial for agricultural
imagery, where early-stage diseases manifest as minute, localized texture variations. An
input image I is processed to generate a feature map where each vector corresponds to a
specific patch of the input image. This ensures that the receptive field is tightly controlled,
allowing for the precise localization of anomalies. The teacher networkT (the PDN) extracts
feature T(I) from the normal training image.

3.3. Evaluation Metrics
The confusion matrix is a standard tool for evaluating the performance of classification
models, utilizing true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), and false nega-
tive (FN) metrics to calculate various quantitative measures. In anomaly detection tasks,
the number of normal samples typically far exceeds that of anomalous samples, resulting in
highly imbalanced datasets. This imbalance biases conventional evaluation metrics, such as
accuracy, making them ineffective. For instance, a model might classify a majority of inputs
as normal, achieving high accuracy while completely failing to identify anomalies. To
address this issue, this study employed the area under the receiver operating characteristic
curve (AUROC) and the area under the per-region overlap curve (AUPRO) as the primary
evaluation metrics for image-level classification and pixel-level localization, respectively.
3.3.1. Image-Level Evaluation (AUROC)
The AUROC is highly effective for binary classification on imbalanced datasets because
it evaluates model performance across all possible decision thresholds. It quantifies the
trade-off between the true positive rate (TPR, or sensitivity) and the false positive rate (FPR,
or 1—specificity). These are calculated as follows:
TPR = TP
TP + FN (8)
FPR = FP
FP + TN (9)
To construct the ROC curve, all possible probability thresholds (0 to 1) are applied
to the anomaly scores, plotting FPR on the x-axis and TPR on the y-axis. The AUROC
represents the integral of this curve, yielding a value between 0 and 1. An AUROC close
to 1 indicates excellent discriminative ability, while a value of 0.5 suggests that the model
performs no better than random guessing.

To ensure a fair and consistent evaluation, the EfficientAD framework was imple-
mented based on the lightweight student–teacher and autoencoder architecture. For feature
extraction, the Teacher network utilized a Patch Description Network (PDN) distilled
from a WideResNet-101 model pretrained on the ImageNet dataset. The Student network
mirrored the convolutional architecture of the Teacher but was initialized randomly. To
capture global logical constraints, the Autoencoder was configured with an information
bottleneck to compress and reconstruct the Teacher’s spatial feature maps.
Input images across all datasets were resized to 256× 256 pixels during preprocessing,
and the PDN was configured to evaluate localized patches of 33× 33 pixels. The training
was optimized using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10−4 and a weight decay
of 10−5. To align with standard label-free anomaly detection protocols and ensure robust
convergence, the model was trained without complex data augmentation. As reflected in
the training loss curves (Figure 2), each subset underwent training for 70,000 iterations
with a batch size of 1. During inference, anomaly maps were generated by normalizing and
combining the local anomaly map (derived from the student–teacher squared difference)
and the global anomaly map (derived from the autoencoder reconstruction error) using a
predefined quantile-based normalization.

superior performance compared to identifying methods, achieving a mean AUROC of
98.6% (EfficientAD-S). It demonstrated exceptional robustness, achieving 100% AUROC

proaches (AnoGAN, Pix2Pix), which exhibited training instability and lower performance
in complex texture categories (e.g., “Grid” and “Wood”), EfficientAD maintained consistent
high performance across all categories. For visual examples of the model’s localization
performance on these industrial categories, please refer to Figure A1 in Appendix A. These
results confirm that the student–teacher architecture with the distilled PDN effectively
captures both local and global anomalies. Having validated the model’s capability on
standard industrial benchmarks, we extended the evaluation to agricultural domains to
assess its effectiveness in crop health monitoring.
4.2. Performance on the PlantVillage Dataset
As shown in Figure 2 (training loss), the EfficientAD framework demonstrated highly
stable training dynamics across all subsets. Unlike the fluctuations often observed in
GAN-based methods, the student–teacher loss ( LST) decreased smoothly, and the Au-
toencoder loss (LAE) remained consistently low, demonstrating stable convergence within
the first 5000 iterations for categories such as “Arjun”, “Pongamia”, and “Potato”. The
“Grape” subset, which previously posed challenges for other architectures, showed stable
convergence without significant loss spikes.
During testing and inference, EfficientAD demonstrated outstanding performance,
achieving perfect or near-perfect results across most categories. As indicated in the results
(Table 3), the model attained an AUROC of 100.0% in “Pongamia” and 99.0% in “Potato”,
underscoring its exceptional adaptability for detecting distinct crop disease features. While
the “Apple” category presented a slight challenge (AUROC 77.5%), likely due to lower
contrast between early-stage lesions and the leaf surface, the overall performance remained
superior to comparative methods. Figure 3 illustrates selected results from EfficientAD
applied to the PlantVillage dataset, highlighting the model’s ability to accurately localize
lesions in the anomaly maps while suppressing noise in the healthy background regions.
While PlantVillage images are acquired under controlled, laboratory-like conditions, real
field environments feature complex backgrounds and varying illumination. To bridge this
gap, we constructed and evaluated a custom real-world Sweet Potato dataset in Section 4.5.

The performance improvements achieved by the BGRP-G strategy were remarkable.
As updated in Table 5, the overall image-level AUROC surged to 99.5%, and the pixel-level

out-of-distribution color masking is a highly effective, data-centric optimization strategy
for label-free anomaly detection in agriculture.

roles of the Autoencoder (AE) and Student–Teacher (S–T) modules within the EfficientAD

4.6. Computational Efficiency and Deployment Feasibility
A critical requirement for automated agricultural sorting lines is high-throughput,
real-time inference. While the core models were trained on a consumer-grade RTX 4070
(Section 3.3), we sought to evaluate the absolute deployment potential of the adapted
EfficientAD framework. To align our benchmark methodology with the original authors
(who tested on RTX A6000/A5000 architectures) and to simulate high-end industrial edge
environments, inference evaluations were additionally conducted using the next-generation
NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada and RTX 5000 Ada GPUs.
As detailed in Table 7, the framework demonstrates an exceptionally lightweight
footprint. The “S” model requires only 8.06 M parameters and 104.04 MiB of GPU memory,
operating at just 75.94 GFLOPs. Even the higher-capacity “M” model remains highly
efficient, utilizing 20.74 M parameters and 227.10 MiB of GPU memory while operating at
235.47 GFLOPs. During inference, the “S” model achieved a striking average latency of
just 1.153 ms per image on the RTX 6000 Ada, translating to a remarkable throughput of
867 frames per second (FPS), and maintained a high speed of 476 FPS on the RTX 5000 Ada.
Notably, even with the increased complexity of the “M” model, the framework still achieved
an impressive latency of 2.255 ms (443 FPS) on the RTX 6000 Ada and 3.334 ms (299 FPS)
on the RTX 5000 Ada. These benchmark results confirm that the proposed label-free
framework completely eliminates the computational bottlenecks typically associated with
heavy supervised detection models, making it exceptionally well-suited for deployment on
resource-constrained Edge AI devices and real-time agricultural inspection systems.

5. Conclusions
This study successfully applied industrial anomaly detection methods to the field of
agricultural imagery. By leveraging pretrained networks for feature extraction and incor-
porating feature transformation adjustments, the extracted features were more accurately
aligned with the target task’s data distribution. This study established and validated a
comprehensive process for anomaly detection and localization across multiple agricul-
tural species. A systematic comparative analysis of various anomaly detection models
was conducted using representative datasets, including MVTec AD, PlantVillage, and
the Coffee Leaf Disease dataset, to evaluate the performance of Teacher–Student, InTra,
AnoGAN, Pix2Pix GAN, and EfficientAD. The results from the training and inference
phases demonstrated that the adapted EfficientAD framework exhibited superior and
consistent performance across most datasets and categories. Its simplified yet effective
student–teacher structure achieved strong generalization capabilities without excessive
reliance on complex data augmentation techniques.
Compared to prevailing paradigms in the existing agricultural deep learning literature
that heavily rely on supervised convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and object detectors
(e.g., ResNet, YOLO), the EfficientAD framework demonstrates three distinct advantages.
First, it operates entirely label-free, circumventing the massive labor costs associated with
pixel-level or bounding-box annotations required by supervised models. Second, it exhibits
exceptional viability on small datasets; while existing studies typically require thousands
of annotated diseased images to prevent overfitting, the framework achieved near-perfect
AUROC scores (up to 99.5%) using only 100 to 200 normal training samples. Finally, unlike
computationally heavy multi-stage detection pipelines, the adapted framework operates
with an exceptionally lightweight footprint (as low as 8.06 M parameters), making it highly
advantageous for the current landscape of smart agriculture.
An analysis of the training process revealed that EfficientAD’s training loss, TPR, and
TNR followed a highly stable trend. Although some subsets exhibited occasional minor
fluctuations in the loss function during early training, the model was able to quickly adjust
and achieve effective convergence, including the model’s robust capability in distinguish-
ing between normal and anomalous samples. At the image-level (AUROC), the framework
demonstrated near-perfect identification of anomalies across different domains. How-
ever, regarding fine-grained regional localization (AUPRO), values were more sensitive
to categories with complex backgrounds, specular reflections, or irregular lesion shapes
(e.g., Coffee Leaf and early-stage Apple diseases). To overcome these challenges posed by
waxy and reflective agricultural surfaces, future feature extractors could integrate spatial
attention mechanisms designed to identify and mask high-intensity specular highlights dy-
namically. Furthermore, at the data-acquisition level, utilizing cross-polarization imaging
could physically suppress reflections.
To further test practical applicability, a background interference analysis utilizing
a custom Sweet Potato dataset revealed that while background noise does not hinder
image-level classification, background-removal preprocessing (BGRM) is a critical step
for reducing false-positive pixel activations and improving the precision of defect bound-
https://doi.org/10.3390/agriculture16080854

=== PAGE BREAK ===

Agriculture 2026, 16, 854 21 of 25
ary localization. This study demonstrates the advantages of label-free frameworks in
anomaly detection tasks, highlighting their potential as simplified, high-efficiency models.
Given its millisecond-level latency and lack of reliance on massive labeled datasets, the
adapted framework holds significant promise for deployment on resource-constrained
Edge AI devices.
While the current model performs exceptionally well on sharp texture discontinuities,
it is sensitive to “blurry” or “gradual” lesion boundaries (such as Black Rot). Future research
should focus on optimizing the student–teacher network by integrating multi-scale feature
fusion to capture broader contextual information around blurry edges, and implementing
boundary-aware loss functions to force attention on soft gradient transitions. Furthermore,
to bridge the gap between controlled laboratory datasets and realistic field conditions
with complex backgrounds and occlusions, incorporating domain adaptation strategies
and multi-modal data (e.g., multispectral imagery) will be crucial for advancing practical,
end-to-end anomaly detection technologies in smart agriculture.
