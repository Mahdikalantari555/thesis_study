1. Introduction 
Rice serves as a staple food for more than 50% of the global population and is fundamental 
to food security, particularly in Asia [ 1 ]. However, its productivity is highly vulnerable to cli- 
mate change [ 2 ]. Crop phenology—the timing of key developmental stages—serves as a primary 
integrator of environmental conditions, directly governing photosynthetic capacity, biomass ac- 
cumulation, and ultimate yield formation [ 3 ]. Consequently, long-term, high-ﬁdelity phenological 
records are not merely observational data; they are essential empirical constraints for validat- 
ing and improving the next generation of Earth System Models (ESMs) and process-based crop 
models. They provide the "ﬁngerprint” of past system behavior against which model skill is mea- 
sured and future projections are calibrated, serving as a critical tool to reduce the "phenological 
uncertainty” in climate impact assessments. 
Phenology represents one of the most sensitive biological indicators of crop responses to cli- 
matic variations. The past four decades has witnessed accelerated global warming and increasing 
frequency of extreme weather events [ 2 , 4 , 5 , 6 ]. Examining long-term trends in the progression of 
rice phenological stages and its duration during this period enables mechanistic quantiﬁcation 
of climatic drivers (including temperature, precipitation, and solar radiation) on crop phenology, 
thereby establishing an empirical foundation for comprehensive impact assessments of climate 
change on agricultural systems [ 6 ]. Furthermore, analyzing long-term variations in rice phenol- 
ogy and its duration facilitates optimization of agricultural production zoning and varietal adap- 
tation. This provides data-driven support for redeﬁning cultivation suitability domains, breeding 
stress-resilient cultivars with climate-synchronized growth cycles, and enhancing resource-use 
eﬃciency [ 7 ]. The integration of multi-decadal crop phenology datasets enables construction of 
“pheno-climatic-yield” nexus models, which project risks of phenological phase shifts under fu- 
ture climate scenarios. Such predictive capacity allows proactive formulation of adaptive inter- 
ventions (e.g., optimized sowing windows and precision ﬁeld management), thereby generating 
targeted policy solutions for smart agriculture and ensuring sustained yield enhancement. 
In China, rice cultivation spans vast areas, divided into 6 major rice cultivation regions and 16 
sub-regions [ 8 , 9 ]. The Middle and Lower Yangtze River Plain (MLYRP) accounts for over one-third 
of China’s total rice cultivation area [ 10 ]. Through the synergistic integration of natural endow- 
ments, historical legacy, and technological innovations, this region has evolved into not merely 
China’s "strategic agricultural nexus" but also serves as a living heritage of global paddy civiliza- 
tion and a critical paradigm for sustainable agricultural intensiﬁcation [ 11 ]. High rice yield acts 
as a cornerstone for food security in China and globally [ 12 ], holding signiﬁcant strategic impor-

tance for ensuring national food security. Changes in rice developmental stages directly impact 
photosynthesis, thereby inﬂuencing grain quality and yield [ 13 ]. Studying these developmental 
shifts is critical for rational agricultural planning, growth regulation, prevention of agricultural 
meteorological disasters, and yield prediction [ 3 ]. Variations in crop developmental stages—such 
as earlier or delayed phenology, shortened or extended growth periods—affect biomass accumu- 
lation time, ultimately threatening crop productivity and food security [ 14–16 ]. 
Understanding climate-phenology interactions here has profound implications for regional 
and global food security. Despite its importance, the research community has been hampered 
by a persistent data bottleneck. Existing phenological resources are often characterized by one 
or more critical limitations: (i) temporal fragmentation, lacking the continuous, > 40-year span 
needed to discern robust climate-driven trends; (ii) phenological incompleteness, focusing on a 
few key stages (e.g., heading, maturity) while omitting the full developmental sequence from 
sowing to maturity required for comprehensive model calibration. 
Our dataset, spanning 95 stations over 44 years with full statistical outputs, is engineered 
speciﬁcally to dismantle this barrier. By providing pre-computed, machine-readable trend statis- 
tics for every station and phenological stage, we address the need for analysis-ready data prod- 
ucts that enable immediate reuse without extensive preprocessing. We move beyond presenting 
raw observations to delivering a curated, analysis-ready data product. Our primary innovation 
lies in bundling intuitive visualizations with a machine-readable repository of pre-computed 
statistical trends for every phenological metric at each station. This dual offering transforms 
the dataset from a static archive into a dynamic, foundational resource. It is purpose-built to 
serve as a community-wide benchmark for validating and improving process-based crop and 
land surface models, for conducting eﬃcient large-scale meta-analyses, and for informing local- 
ized adaptation strategies with station-speciﬁc evidence. This paper details the dataset’s com- 
pilation, rigorous quality control, structure, and its multifaceted potential for reuse across the 
Earth system sciences. 
Unlike previous effort s, the primary innovation of this dataset lies in transforming raw ob- 
servations into a readily analyzable data product. This is achieved through two key offerings: 
1) a curated collection of high-resolution visualizations depicting spatiotemporal patterns, and 
2) a machine-readable Excel table containing all linear trend parameters, which elevates the 
dataset from a mere collection of ﬁgures to a foundational resource for quantitative analysis. 
The outcomes establish a robust theoretical framework for understanding climate-crop interac- 
tions while providing empirical support for optimizing cultivation management strategies. This 
investigation delivers a dataset that transcends previous efforts by offering an unprecedented, 
ready-to-use resource of spatially explicit, data-driven insights, thereby directly addressing the 
data gap and empowering the research community with a foundational tool for climate adapta- 
tion research. 
2. Data and Methods 
2.1. Study region and agrometeorological station network 
Our dataset includes the records collected from 1981 to 2024 of 95 agrometeorological obser- 
vation stations in the MLYRP, including Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui, Jiangxi, Hubei, Hunan provinces, 
and Shanghai Municipalities. These stations were maintained by the Chinese Meteorological Ad- 
ministration to investigate local crop production and agricultural meteorological disasters, as 
well as provide suggestions for local farmers to manage agricultural meteorological disasters. 
There were 1 station in Shanghai Municipalities, 8 stations in Jiangsu province, 15 stations in 
Zhejiang province, 11 stations in Anhui province, 16 stations in Jiangxi province, 21 stations in 
Hubei province, 23 stations in Hunan province ( Fig. 1 ). 
All stations are located in a rice primary production region, and represent the typical crop- 
ping system in the MLYRP. They are geographically and climatologically different, and have good

2.2. Dataset construction workﬂow 
The dataset construction followed a structured pipeline ( Fig. 2 ) designed to ensure robust- 
ness and reproducibility: 
(1) Data collection: Historical data and electronic data were collected from oﬃcial archives 
and oﬃcial website of Chinese Meteorological Administration. The detailed phenology records 
from 1981 to 2024 included the dates of rice sowing (SO), emergence (EM), trefoil (TS), trans- 
planting (TR), regreening (RE), tillering (TI), jointing (JO), booting (BO), heading (HE), milking 
(MI) and maturity (MA). 
(2) Data processing quality control: A multi-tiered quality control protocol was imple- 
mented: 
Firstly, the collected data are categorized by year, site, crop, and phenology. 
Then, A multi-step quality control protocol was implemented to identify and handle potential 
anomalies and outliers: 
Systemic Factor Screening: Station metadata and historical records were consulted to identify 
periods affected by non-climatic factors (e.g., station relocations, changes in observation proto- 
cols). Data from these periods were ﬂagged for expert validation. 
Statistical Thresholding: For each phenological stage at each individual station, observations 
falling beyond three standard deviations ( ±3 σ) from the long-term mean were automatically 
ﬂagged as potential outliers. 
Expert Validation: Automatically ﬂagged values were not removed arbitrarily. Instead, 
they were rigorously examined by agrometeorological experts. This involved cross-referencing 
with adjacent stations to check for spatial coherence (suggesting a climatic extreme) and 
consulting original logbooks for notations. Only values conclusively deemed erroneous af- 
ter this contextual analysis were corrected or excluded, preserving legitimate climatic 
extremes.

Finally, the validated dates were converted to day of year (DOY), calculation of 
growth period length (GLs), and statistical analysis (e.g., mean and standard deviation

We assigned January 1 of each calendar year a reference value of 1, subsequently transform-

rigorous visual representations in our dataset compilation. This methodology enabled robust 
estimation of population characteristics from sample observations, outperforming conventional 
histogram-based methods through its continuous density estimation independent of arbitrary

Univariate linear regression: Phenology trend analysis was conducted through ﬁrst-order 
linear regression modeling, with temporal patterns in DOY and GLs quantiﬁed using slope coef- 
ﬁcients. This method was selected to provide a clear, robust, and interpretable estimate of the 
overall net change over the 44-year study period, which is a community-standard approach for 
establishing a baseline understanding in climatological and phenological studies. The regression 
parameters were estimated via ordinary least squares (OLS) optimization, expressed as: 
Y = kx + b (3) 
Where Y denotes either DOY or GLs measurements, and x represents calendar year; b represents 
the y-intercept; k represents the annual rate of change. Decadal trends were derived as 10 ×k , 
with negative coeﬃcients indicating phenology date advancement/GLs contraction and positive 
values signifying phenology date delay or GLs extension. Two tailed t-test analysis was used to 
determine the statistical signiﬁcance of the change trend. The signiﬁcance level for all regression 
analyses was set at α = 0.05. The regression analysis revealed p-values < 0.01 for key variables. 
2.2.1. Final product curation 
Kernel density estimation (KDE) and linear regression were systematically executed in Orig- 
inPro (Version 2024). The primary purpose of these analyses was to generate the standard- 
ized graphical and numerical outputs that constitute the core of this dataset. The outputs—
2,432 high-resolution KDE and trend graphs (JPEG) and the comprehensive statistical spread- 
sheet (XLSX)—were organized into a structured repository. 
All resulting statistical parameters from the linear regression (including province, station ID, 
station name, crop types, phenology period, slope (variation trends), intercept, R2 , model mean 
square, F-value, and p-value) for every phenological stage and growth period length at each sta- 
tion were compiled into a comprehensive Excel spreadsheet, forming a key part of the published 
data product.

3. Technical Validation 
To ensure the dataset meets the highest standards of reliability and ﬁtness for reuse, we 
implemented a comprehensive, multi-layered validation framework. This framework assesses the 
entire data pipeline, from the foundational raw observations to the ﬁnal derived data products 
(statistical trends and visualizations). 
3.1. Foundational data integrity and quality control 
The integrity of this dataset is rooted in China’s nationally standardized agricultural meteo- 
rological observation network, which provides the underlying raw data. 
• Observational Standardization: All phenological data were collected in strict adherence to 
the national "Speciﬁcation for Agrometeorological Observation” [ 19 ]. Observations were gen- 
erally conducted every two days during the growing season, with daily observations during 
critical stages such as heading and ﬂowering to ensure accurate recording of these short- 
duration events. This process was carried out by a professional workforce of over 100 certi- 
ﬁed technicians, 82% of whom possess more than a decade of ﬁeld experience, ensuring high 
consistency and expert judgment across the network. 
• Systematic Quality Assurance Protocol: A rigorous, multi-step quality control (QC) pipeline 
was applied to the raw observational records: 
∗ Automated Checks: Data underwent automated screening for completeness, duplication, 
valid station IDs, temporal coherence, and value range plausibility. 
∗ Expert-Driven Validation: Potential outliers ﬂagged by statistical thresholds (e.g., values 
beyond ±3 σ from the long-term mean) were not automatically discarded. Instead, they 
underwent expert arbitration, which involved cross-referencing with adjacent stations for 
spatial coherence and consulting original logbooks to distinguish genuine climatic ex- 
tremes from errors. 
∗ Gap-ﬁlling and Correction: Anomalies and missing values were addressed using a hierar- 
chical protocol. First, all ﬂagged records were checked against original paper logbooks and 
oﬃcial electronic archives. Second, for isolated missing values at a station, the value was 
estimated from the same station’s adjacent years (previous and following years) when the 
cropping system and cultivar group were unchanged; for phenological dates, the mean of 
the nearest available years was used. Third, for consecutive missing years, we referred 
to neighboring stations with the same rice cropping system and similar agroclimatic 
conditions to evaluate spatial coherence, and the ﬁnal value was determined by expert 
agronomic judgment. Fourth, outliers beyond ±3 σ were not automatically removed; they 
were retained as genuine climatic extremes when supported by neighboring stations and 
logbooks, and were corrected or excluded only when conclusively identiﬁed as errors.

3.2. Validation of derived data products 
The robustness of the core data products—the linear trend statistics and kernel density esti- 
mation (KDE) visualizations—was rigorously tested. 
• Validation of Trend Statistics (Linear Regression): The ordinary least squares (OLS) linear 
regression used to generate decadal trends was subjected to two key validation steps: 
∗ Residual Analysis: An analysis of residuals conﬁrmed that the key OLS assumptions (nor- 
mality, independence, and homoscedasticity) were not substantially violated, supporting 
the validity of the ﬁtted models. 
∗ Robustness to Outliers (Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation): To ensure the derived trends 
were not unduly inﬂuenced by individual extreme years, we employed a leave-one-out 
cross-validation (LOOCV) approach. This process systematically recalculated the linear 
trend after excluding each year from the 44-year series. The results demonstrated excep- 
tional stability, with slope coeﬃcients varying by less than ±0.1 days per decade across 
all iterations, conﬁrming the robustness of the reported trends. 
• Sensitivity Analysis of Phenological Distributions (KDE): The KDE plots, which visualize the 
probability distribution of phenological timing, are sensitive to the selection of bandwidth. 
For the ﬁnal published KDE products, Scott’s rule was used. To verify that the observed pat- 
terns were genuine features of the data and not artifacts of the algorithm, we conducted a

sensitivity analysis. We recomputed all densities using both Scott’s rule and Silverman’s rule 
of thumb. The resulting probability density functions showed consistent modal peaks and 
distribution shapes across both methods, conﬁrming the reliability of the visualized patterns. 
The ﬁnal published KDE ﬁgures and statistics are based on Scott’s rule. 
3.3. Intrinsic data quality and uncertainty assessment 
• Uncertainty Sources and Mitigation: We acknowledge and have mitigated several potential 
sources of observational uncertainty: 
∗ Human Judgment: Minimized through standardized training and expert arbitration. 
∗ Environmental Interference: Addressed via expert annotation and contextual validation. 
∗ Instrumental Limitations: Mitigated by tool calibration and the use of relative, observer- 
deﬁned phenological criteria. 
∗ Historical Data Gaps: Filled using statistically and experientially informed imputation 
methods. 
• Intrinsic Consistency as a Quality Metric: The statistical properties of the ﬁnal dataset itself 
provide compelling evidence of its high quality. The widespread high statistical signiﬁcance 
(p < 0.01 for 78% of phenological stages) and substantial explanatory power (R ² > 0.5 for key 
developmental transitions like transplanting and tillering) observed across the extensive sta- 
tion network are indicative of an internally consistent and climatically sensitive dataset. Such 
strong, coherent signals would be unlikely to arise from a noisy or unreliable data source. 
In summary, this comprehensive technical validation—spanning foundational QC, product 
robustness checks, and intrinsic consistency assessment—aﬃrms that the spatiotemporal pat- 
terns and statistical derivatives presented in this dataset are robust and statistically sound. This 
provides end-users with a high-conﬁdence foundation for subsequent scientiﬁc analysis and 
decision-making.

5. Dataset Values and Potential for Reuse 
This dataset provides an analysis-ready resource of rice phenology trends across China’s pri- 
mary rice-producing region. By delivering pre-computed statistical derivatives alongside high- 
resolution visualizations, it lowers the barrier for diverse applications. Researchers can use the 
station-level trend statistics to benchmark crop and land surface models, conduct meta-analyses 
of climate impacts across geographical and management gradients, and validate long-term phe- 
nological trends derived from remote sensing products. The structured format enables eﬃcient 
data extraction for large-scale synthesis, while the visualization collection facilitates intuitive 
exploration of spatiotemporal patterns. For policymakers and agricultural extension services, the 
dataset offers evidence-based insights for adaptation planning—such as optimizing planting cal- 
endars and selecting climate-adapted cultivars based on observed rates of phenological change. 
Educators can leverage the integrated framework as a teaching tool for climate impact assess- 
ment and data analysis. Ultimately, this dataset serves as a foundational community resource 
for advancing research on climate-crop interactions and supporting data-informed decisions to 
enhance agricultural resilience and food security.
