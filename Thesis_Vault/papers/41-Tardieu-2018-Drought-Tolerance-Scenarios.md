## Corpus & Scope

- Invited *Annual Review* article (plant physiology → breeding). Structure: water deficit as supply/demand imbalance → five short-term feedback processes (minutes-to-hours) → long-term plant-intrinsic properties (months) → Table 1 trait × scenario matrix → a probabilistic pipeline → 8 summary points.
- Evidence base is **meta-analyses of field experiments**: 19 maize field experiments across Europe (Fig. 4a), 18 wheat field experiments in Australia and Mexico (Fig. 4b), and a 29-experiment European maize meta-analysis (pp. 745, 748).

## What It Does

Argues that **drought tolerance is not a fixed trait but a probabilistic, scenario-conditioned property**. It separates short-term physiological feedbacks (stomatal control, tissue hydraulic conductance, osmotic adjustment, growth, carbon/water optimisation) that stabilise shoot water potential within minutes from long-term developmental strategies (cycle duration, grain abortion, root architecture, stay-green) operating over the crop cycle. Because the same trait can raise or lower yield depending on the scenario, it estimates the *benefit and risk* of each combination of alleles per scenario via phenotyping, field meta-analysis, modelling and genomic prediction, instead of ranking genotypes on one tolerance scale.

## Key Methodology

- Short-term feedbacks are framed mechanistically with the hydraulics variables named: leaf/root **water potential** (Ψ_bulk, Ψ_rhizo, Ψ_xyl, Ψ_bundle, Ψ_growth, Ψ_mature — Fig. 1, p. 735), tissue **hydraulic conductance** and **hydraulic capacitance**, **guard-cell turgor**, **ABA**, **PIP aquaporin** transcript abundance, **osmotic potential** and turgor, plus leaf/root growth rates.
- Traits are scored against five **environmental scenarios** defined by soil-water-deficit pattern (early vs terminal, mild vs severe), evaporative demand (ET), air temperature (T) and soil depth (Table 1, p. 741).
- Effect sizes from QTL meta-analyses; proposed pipeline: top-down QTL identification from tens of field experiments → bottom-up phenomic characterisation of traits → joint analysis predicting traits and yield of any related genotype in multiple scenarios → multiyear multisite simulation of virtual genotypes → comparison with field experiments (pp. 749–750).

## Key Findings

- **A scenario is a *time course*, not a place.** "Environmental scenario: a clustered pattern of time courses of soil water status, evaporative demand, light, and temperature in different fields; **a given field can belong to different scenarios in different years**" (p. 736). And: "experiments were clustered into environmental scenarios on the basis of **measured environmental conditions rather than geographic locations** because geography alone failed to provide any insight into the conditional effects of QTLs" — fields a short distance apart, "or even the same field during two different years", were classified under different scenarios because of rainfall variability (pp. 748–749). → *A stress class is a property of (field, time window), not of a field.* The most thesis-relevant statement in the paper.
- **Trait effects flip sign with the scenario.** "Most traits, either constitutive or resulting from adaptive processes, show different effects on growth and yield depending on environmental scenarios … all traits could have positive or negative effects on yield depending on the scenario" (p. 747); "we are not aware of any trait, even considered positive and scalable in a given environment, whose effect would not be negative in other environments" (p. 748). Summary point 5: "A given trait can therefore be related to yield with positive or negative correlations depending on drought scenarios, which are defined by the clustering of time courses of environmental conditions" (p. 751).
- **The sign flips are documented trait by trait** (Table 1, p. 741; five scenarios = early WD/mild ET/shallow soil → terminal WD/high ET/shallow soil → terminal WD/high ET/deep soil → mild WD/high ET/high T/deep soil → mild WD/high ET/high T/shallow soil): stringent stomatal control (−, +, =/+, −, −); high hydraulic conductance (+, −, +, ++, −); deep rooting (−, −, +, +, −); high intrinsic transpiration (=, −, −, ++, −); early flowering (=, +, +, −, +/=); sensitive grain abortion (−, −, +, +, −); upper-layer root efficiency (+, −, −, +, +); CAM (−, =/+, −, −, −); plus stay-green and water-use-efficiency as *integrated* traits. Authors' caveat: "the evaluation of positive and negative effects is based on simulations and expert diagnosis and therefore can be discussed" (p. 741). **Canopy temperature (CT) is listed as the efficient phenotyping technique** for stringent stomatal control, high transpiration, CAM and glaucousness — independent support for CT/CWSI as a *feature*, not only an output.
- **Allele-level reliability caps hard labels.** "In meta-analyses of field experiments, a given allele has a positive effect in at most half of the fields, and usually for much smaller proportions" (p. 748). In the 29-experiment maize meta-analysis "nearly all QTLs had conditional effects upon yield that were **positive, negative, or null** depending on environmental conditions at flowering time" (p. 748); Fig. 4a plots maize allelic effects over roughly −5 % to +15 %, Fig. 4b a wheat allelic effect on grain yield of about −0.1 to +0.4 t ha⁻¹. A binary label therefore carries ≲50 % correctness by construction; a soft/probabilistic label is the statistically honest encoding.
- **Osmotic adjustment decouples turgor from function — the link to salinity.** "The turgor of roots and shoots … can show little or no variation even when the water potential of these organs varies by **0.5 MPa**"; osmotic adjustment is "rapid, with a typical halftime of **30 min**"; yet "growth or photosynthesis remain affected by water deficit **even when the turgor of leaf cells is maintained** via osmotic adjustment", which "may explain why the genetic variability in the ability of plants to adjust osmotically often does not correlate with the long-term consequences of drought on plant growth and yield" (p. 740). → Under osmotic (e.g. salinity) stress a plant can hold turgor — and hence canopy temperature — near normal while water status and carbon assimilation deteriorate: the thermal channel is not merely noisy under salinity, it is **structurally biased**.
- **The hydraulics that can break the ambiguity are named.** Hydropassive vs hydroactive closure; isohydric (maize, poplar) vs anisohydric (sunflower) behaviour and a genetic continuum within a species (p. 739); hydraulics as primary message with ABA secondary or the reverse, depending on species (pp. 739–740); ABA raises root hydraulic conductance while lowering transpiring-leaf conductance; PIP aquaporins; root hydraulic conductance follows a circadian rhythm with a morning maximum whose amplitude increases after recent water deficit and high evaporative demand (pp. 740–741).
- **Growth is a separate, thermal-safe channel.** Leaf expansion nearly ceases within ~30 min of the early-morning transpiration rise, and irrigation restores leaf growth within 45 min (p. 742); explicitly "**reducing leaf area is not associated with a risk of heat stress**", unlike stomatal closure, which causes leaf heating (p. 742) → an LAI/optical trajectory separates "less leaf area" from "closed stomata" and from "heat load", which a thermal index cannot.
- **Timescales make single-date labels unsound.** Water deficit is defined over minutes-to-hours, yield over months; during one summer day "a plant can register **11 °C** in the early morning with a VPD close to **0 kPa** and show a favorable water status, but experience **36 °C** with a VPD of **3.5 kPa** and suffer severe symptoms of water stress **six hours later**" (p. 737); leaf/stem water potential can fluctuate by **1 MPa over 1 h** in a diurnal cycle (p. 737). Because mechanisms "interact and act as feedback, causes and effects cannot emerge directly from intuitive extrapolations and instead require tools that allow integration over time" (p. 744).
- **Scale of the prize:** genetic progress under water deficit is 19 and 80 kg ha⁻¹ year⁻¹ in wheat and maize; released drought-tolerant transgenic plants give 2–4 % yield gains, within the range of conventional breeding (p. 747).
- ⚠️ **Discrepancy with intake note A3:** the note's relevance is "Medium–High" while the matrix row says **Medium** — the matrix verdict is kept. Substantively the note is accurate; it omits that the paper's probability is over **allele combinations and yield across breeding scenarios**, not over per-observation stress classes — the transfer to a soft *remote-sensing* label is an analogy, not a result of the paper.

## Relevance to Thesis

**Medium** — label-design rationale: trait ranking and even the *sign* of trait effects flip with the drought scenario, and scenario membership is defined by measured environmental time-courses, which motivates soft/mixture labels conditioned on the observation window over static global stress classes. It is a breeding/physiology framework, not an RS/ML label protocol.
- ✅ Supplies the traits and hydraulics variables a soft label could be conditioned on: isohydry/anisohydry, guard-cell turgor, root vs leaf hydraulic conductance, PIP aquaporins, ABA, osmotic adjustment, leaf-area growth — and lists **canopy temperature** as the accepted high-throughput proxy for the stomatal-control traits (Table 1, p. 741).
- ✅ "A given field can belong to different scenarios in different years" (p. 736) and clustering by *measured conditions rather than geography* (pp. 748–749) → support for per-scene, time-window-conditioned labels over per-field static labels, and for the multi-temporal SITS premise.
- ✅ Osmotic adjustment buffers turgor with a ~30 min halftime while growth and photosynthesis still deteriorate (p. 740) → the mechanism by which salinity-driven stress can leave CWSI near normal: a systematic, not stochastic, bias in thermal-only labelling.
- ✅ The "reducing leaf area is not associated with a risk of heat stress" contrast (p. 742) identifies a candidate optical/LAI discriminator against the heat confound of #40.
- ⚠️ The paper's probability is over allele combinations and yield, not per-pixel stress classes; it offers a *principle* for soft labels, not a label protocol, and contains no EO/ML content.
- ⚠️ The osmotic-adjustment argument is about drought; the paper has **no salinity treatment**, so extending it to salinity is an explicit inference.
- ❌ No remote sensing, no sugarcane, no salinity experiment, no label definitions, no sequence model, no validation protocol.

## Key Takeaway

Two things are worth taking from Tardieu: a **scenario is a time-course of measured conditions, not a place** (p. 736, pp. 748–749), so a stress class belongs to a (field, window) pair — exactly the premise of a multi-temporal SITS design; and **osmotic adjustment keeps turgor (hence canopy temperature) near normal while water status and carbon assimilation deteriorate** (p. 740), a mechanism — not noise — by which thermal-only labels are blind to salinity-driven stress. Caveat: the "probabilistic" framing is a breeding decision framework over allele × scenario, so the soft-label transfer is an analogy the thesis must operationalise itself.
