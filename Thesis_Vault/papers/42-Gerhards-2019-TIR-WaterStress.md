## What It Does

Reviews crop water-stress detection from TIR multi-/hyperspectral imagery, from SIF and from VNIR/SWIR hyperspectral imagery, and argues that no single spectral domain is sufficient. It works through the **TIR physics** (radiance → surface temperature + spectral emissivity, temperature–emissivity separation, atmospheric correction), catalogues the temperature-based indices (SDD, CWSI, WDI, I_G) next to the optical and fluorescence indices, inventories available and planned sensors, and closes with the multi-sensor argument and the four challenges blocking operational use of hyperspectral TIR for precision agriculture.

## Key Methodology

- **TIR measurement physics:** TIR radiance carries two intrinsic quantities — the directional radiometric surface temperature and the spectral emissivity (p. 4). Two problems must be solved: atmospheric correction (down-welling radiance, up-welling radiance, transmittance τ) and the **temperature–emissivity separation (TES)** — radiance in *n* bands gives *n* + 1 unknowns (*n* emissivities plus surface temperature), an ill-posed problem that only hyperspectral data can regularise (p. 4). Legacy broadband sensors assume constant emissivity (0.97 for vegetation) that "does not exist in nature"; "neglecting the spectral emissivity … an error of 1 % results in absolute temperature errors of about **1 K**" (p. 8).
- **Index construction:** normalisation of leaf temperature against air temperature (SDD, measured 1–1.5 h after solar noon; stress assumed as soon as T_leaf − T_air > 0 K) and against wet/dry reference bounds (CWSI, eq. 1), plus VI-based and energy-balance alternatives (WDI, I_G) (p. 6).
- **Co-occurrence framing:** plant responses to water stress "appear synergistically or antagonistically and are modified by co-occurring plant stresses under field conditions", treated as the reason a single-response detection strategy fails (p. 2; Fig. 1 modified from Jones and Vaughan).
## Key Findings

- **The single-response limitation, stated by the review itself.** "As plant responses to water stress are numerous and complex, they appear synergistically or antagonistically and are modified by **co-occurring plant stresses** under field conditions (Figure 1). Therefore, it remains difficult to detect and monitor plant water deficit **based on a single plant response**" (p. 2) — the same equifinality argument as #39, one level higher (across plant responses rather than across drivers of one response).
- **The TIR water-stress chain:** stomatal closure (triggered by soil-water deficit *or* by high evaporative demand) reduces vapour exchange → reduces evaporative cooling → "an overall increase of plant surface temperature compared to a plant which does not suffer from water stress" (p. 3). Downstream: photosynthesis falls, leaf expansion slows, chlorophyll is lost under persistent deficit, and SIF decreases while non-photochemical quenching increases (p. 3). Osmotic adjustment (solute accumulation to hold turgor) is listed as a further strategy against irreversible damage (p. 3).
- **CWSI (eq. 1) and its reference problem:** CWSI = (T_leaf − T_wet)/(T_dry − T_wet), T_wet the lower boundary (fully open stomata, maximum potential transpiration), T_dry the upper boundary (non-transpiring). It "implicitly accounts for effects of free convection … and thus indirectly considers not only current air temperature but also other environmental factors (i.e., wind, radiation, VPD, etc.)", so "**CWSI should be adaptable to any crop under any meteorological condition**" (p. 6). References are built analytically (leaf temperature + micrometeorological data) or empirically (leaf temperature, air temperature, VPD + two empirical calibration variables). But "despite CWSI performing reasonably well in dry and hot climates with high VPD, its performance is **limited in humid climates with low VPD** and high variability in wind speed, cloud cover, and thus incident radiation" (p. 6). Artificial reference surfaces fix this (all values inside one image, no extra meteorological measurements) but must share the aerodynamic/optical properties and emissivity of real leaves; "at the airborne and satellite scale, the application of artificial reference surfaces seems to be difficult" and could be replaced by larger wet references (p. 6).
- **Index catalogue (Table 1, p. 7):** TIR — SDD, CWSI, WDI (combines NDVI/SAVI with T_c to separate vegetation from soil pixels), spectral emissivity; VNIR/SWIR — PRI, SR, NDVI, WI, LWI, MSI, NDWI; SIF — SIF_685, SIF_740, SIF_685/SIF_740. "Based on some rearrangement of the leaf energy balance equation, **I_G has the advantage over CWSI that it is directly linearly related to stomatal conductance**" (p. 7).
- **Sensor reality for the thesis's platform (Table 2, pp. 7–8):** Landsat 8 TIRS 10.6–12.5 µm, 2 bands, 100 m GSD, 16-day revisit; Landsat TM 10.4–12.5 µm, 1 band, 120 m, 16 days; ETM+ 60 m, 16 days; Terra/ASTER 8.15–11.65 µm, 5 bands, 90 m, 16 days; NOAA/AVHRR 10.3–12.5 µm, 1090 m, 1 day. "the best spatial resolution with 100 m is delivered by Landsat 8, which for most agricultural cultivation systems corresponds to **a single field per pixel**" (p. 7). Structural trade-off: "current state-of-the-art thermal infrared sensing satellites either deliver high temporal (e.g., MODIS, AVHRR, or Sentinel-3 with **1–3 days** revisit time) but coarse spatial resolution (i.e., **1 km**) or high spatial (e.g., Landsat series, ASTER with ~**100 m**) but low temporal (i.e., **16 days**) resolution images" (p. 15). Remedies listed: pan-sharpening, thermal sharpening, temperature unmixing (p. 15).
- **Multi-sensor synergy is the conclusion.** "Since different plant stresses co-occurring under field conditions cause various plant responses, a multi-sensor approach not only provides useful information about current plant status but also **on the causes** of biophysical, physiological, and photochemical changes" (p. 12); "A **synergistic multi-sensor use will provide better information on the crop water status than the information provided by a single spectral domain**" (p. 16); SAR-derived soil water content (e.g. Sentinel-1) combined with ET modelling adds soil–plant–atmosphere information (p. 15).
- **Counter-nuance to keep honest:** in a comparative airborne study on cereals under different irrigation levels, "temperature-based indices performed **best** in comparison to SIF and optical indices (e.g. PRI)" (p. 12). TIR is the most sensitive single domain for water stress; the failure is **specificity**, not sensitivity.
- **Four scale-up challenges (p. 15):** (i) missing thresholds of temperature-based indices (e.g. CWSI) for irrigation scheduling; (ii) missing knowledge of the relationship between spectral emissivity features and changes in leaf traits under stress; (iii) lack of current TIR satellite missions with suitable spectral and spatial resolution; (iv) lack of appropriate data-processing schemes and radiative-transfer models in the TIR domain (atmospheric correction, temperature–emissivity separation) at airborne and satellite level. Emissivity-based detection was shown at ground level, but "most of the spectral information of plant emissivity spectra is lost through scaling effects (e.g., mixed pixel, cavity effects)" when up-scaled (p. 12).
- Context: agriculture consumes 80–90 % of the freshwater used by humans, about two thirds of it for crop irrigation; +1.0–2.5 °C in annual mean global temperature over the next 50 years (p. 2).
- ⚠️ **Discrepancy with the matrix row:** the row says "single-index CWSI fails under co-occurring stresses". The PDF's own wording is broader and softer — it is "difficult to detect and monitor plant water deficit based on a **single plant response**", with co-occurring stresses modifying responses "synergistically or antagonistically" (p. 2); the CWSI-specific failure it documents is **meteorological** (low VPD, wind/cloud variability, reference-surface construction at scale, p. 6), not a demonstration that CWSI is defeated by salinity or co-occurring stresses. There is **no salinity discussion and no salinity experiment** in the review. "CWSI fails under co-occurring stresses" is a defensible thesis-level inference, not a finding of this paper — cite it as such.
- ⚠️ **Internal tension:** p. 6 says CWSI "should be adaptable to any crop under any meteorological condition" and, in the same paragraph, that its performance "is limited in humid climates with low VPD". The underlying reason is documented in #39: the empirical baselines a_pot/b_pot are not weather-independent.

## Relevance to Thesis

**High** — supplies the TIR physics, index catalogue, sensor inventory and multi-sensor argument that justify the thermal branch and the Landsat-based design, and states the single-response limitation explicitly.
- ✅ TIR water-stress physics (stomatal closure → reduced evaporative cooling → warmer canopy, p. 3) and the CWSI/WDI/I_G formulations (eq. 1, Table 1, pp. 6–7) that back the thesis's thermal features.
- ✅ Sensor-side justification for the thesis's platform: Landsat 8/9 TIRS at 100 m ≈ one field per pixel with 16-day revisit (p. 7), set against the high-temporal/low-spatial trade-off (MODIS, AVHRR, Sentinel-3: 1–3 days, 1 km, p. 15) → justifies a multi-year thermal SITS combined with high-cadence optical.
- ✅ Explicit support for fusing domains rather than reading one index: "synergistic multi-sensor use will provide better information on the crop water status than the information provided by a single spectral domain" (p. 16), with SAR soil water content + ET modelling as the soil-side complement (p. 15).
- ✅ The "single plant response" statement (p. 2) is a citable, review-level formulation of the thesis's concurrent-stress problem.
- ⚠️ The multi-sensor recommendation is TIR + VNIR/SWIR + SIF (+SAR) only — **no machine learning, no self-supervision, no sequence models, no explainability**; the thesis's method layer is outside this paper's scope.
- ⚠️ Emissivity-based detection does not up-scale (p. 12) and CWSI thresholds for irrigation scheduling are still missing (p. 15) → do not over-promise the emissivity branch.
- ❌ No salinity treatment, no sugarcane, no label protocol, no validation design, no XAI.

## Key Takeaway

The review's bottom line is the thesis's design rule: because plant responses to water stress "appear synergistically or antagonistically and are modified by co-occurring plant stresses", "it remains difficult to detect and monitor plant water deficit based on a single plant response" (p. 2) — so a single-index thermal label cannot be expected to separate concurrent water and salinity stress, and multi-sensor (thermal + optical [+ SAR]) integration is required (pp. 12, 16). Caveat: the review's specific CWSI failure is meteorological and it never tests salinity, so "CWSI fails under co-occurring stresses" must be presented as the thesis's inference, not as this paper's finding.
